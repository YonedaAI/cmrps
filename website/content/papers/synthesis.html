Abstract

This paper states the structural picture formed by the twelve parts of the Classification of Mathematical Representations of Physical Systems, a program asking whether mathematical representations of physical systems admit a structural classification analogous to the Classification of Finite Simple Groups. One ontology of forty-two definitions, one dependency graph, and one equivalence discipline run through the series. Three kinds of result organize its classification content. Complete invariants appear wherever the invariant is finite or explicitly parameterized: dimension vectors for finite-dimensional \(C^*\)-algebras, Dynkin and Satake diagrams for semisimple Lie theory, classifying spaces for principal bundles, and the Murray-von Neumann and Connes data for amenable factors. Reconstruction theorems recover objects from probes under hypotheses that cannot be dropped. Seven obstruction theorems bound the scope of any classification, from uncountably many type \(\mathrm{II}_1\) factors to algorithmic unrecognizability in dimension four. Every equivalence in the series declares the structure it preserves, and equivalences that forget the symmetric layer fail in a proved counterexample. The structural classification conjecture is stated as the program’s theorem target with its five proof obligations. The twelve parts share definitions, dependency discipline, and claim vocabulary without conflict.

Introduction

The Classification of Mathematical Representations of Physical Systems asks whether the mathematical structures that represent physical systems admit a structural classification analogous to the Classification of Finite Simple Groups. The twelve parts of the series answer in pieces. This paper states the structural picture those pieces form: one ontology, one dependency discipline, one equivalence requirement, and one register of obstruction theorems, carried across classical mechanics, quantum theory, gauge fields, spacetime, and gravity.

The target of the program is the identity \[\mathrm{Phys}_{\mathrm{adm}} \simeq \langle \mathfrak{F}_\alpha \rangle_{\mathfrak{C}},\] in which every admissible physical system is generated from a collection of fundamental structural families under admissible constructions. The identity is the program’s theorem target and is stated in full in 1. The results around it are of three kinds: classification theorems that supply complete invariants in restricted sectors, reconstruction theorems that recover objects from probes, and obstruction theorems that mark where no classification of the stated generality can exist.

Complete invariants appear exactly where the invariant is finite or explicitly parameterized. Dimension vectors classify finite-dimensional \(C^*\)-algebras [1], Dynkin and Satake diagrams classify complex and real semisimple Lie algebras [2], classifying spaces classify principal bundles [3], [4], and the Murray-von Neumann and Connes data classify amenable factors [5], [6]. Outside those regimes the series records either a parameterized family of answers or an obstruction theorem.

Reconstruction is better developed than classification. Gelfand and Neumark [7], Gelfand duality [8], Stone duality [9], Doplicher and Roberts [10], [11], and Deligne [12] each recover an object from a category of probes, and each does so under hypotheses that cannot be dropped. The symmetric tensor structure, the growth condition, and the commutativity assumption are load-bearing in their respective statements.

Nonclassifiability is a theorem. The construction of uncountably many type \(\mathrm{II}_1\) factors [13], the undecidability results for finitely presented groups [14], the wildness theorems of representation theory [15], [16], [17], and the algorithmic unrecognizability of certain four-manifolds [18] each prove that no classification of the stated kind exists in the stated generality. These results are first-class content of the series. They mark the scope of the classification program rather than its failure.

The shared framework

Every part of the series works in one definitional substrate. The substrate is a category of representations of physical systems, a discipline of morphisms, a physical equivalence, a collection of structural families, and an invariant record. The definitions are stated once in the foundational part and reused across the series; later parts extend the list only by appending new identifiers. The identifiers and their roles appear in 1.

Objects and components

Definition 1 (Representation of a physical system). A representation of a physical system is a tuple \[P = (X, \mathcal{O}, \mathcal{D}, \mathcal{S}, \mathcal{C}),\] carrying a state space or geometric structure, an observable algebra or collection, dynamical laws, symmetry and constraint data, and composition and interaction rules.

The five components of 1 record coordinate data whose concrete realization varies across physical regimes. State spaces instantiate as symplectic manifolds [19], [20], Hilbert spaces [1], probability spaces, smooth manifolds [21], [22], and lattice configurations; observable layers instantiate as function algebras, \(C^*\)-algebras [1], von Neumann algebras [23], and sheaf-theoretic observable atlases. The components need not be independent. The composition component \(\mathcal{C}\) determines the decomposition theory of the representation, because it defines irreducibility as the absence of a nontrivial factorization under the specified composition operation.

Objects assemble into a category \(\mathrm{Phys}\) whose morphisms are admissible relationships between representations. The full subcategory of admissible objects is \(\mathrm{Phys}_{\mathrm{adm}}\), defined in 3. Theorems stated over the admissible subcategory are stated relative to the conditions of that definition.

Morphism kinds

Five kinds of relationship appear across the series, and they are not all arrows of one 1-category. Coarse-graining forgets microscopic detail while preserving macroscopic observables. Exact equivalence is invertible and represents identity of content up to the chosen relation. Embedding realizes a system as a subsystem, a subtheory, or a sub-structure. These three are arrows in the strict reading of \(\mathrm{Phys}\). Duality exchanges structure between differently presented theories and is a functor between category presentations, generally invertible only after passage to an opposite or dual category. Limiting procedures arise from limits and colimits: thermodynamic limits, infinite-volume limits, semiclassical limits, and continuum limits. These last two are universal constructions rather than arrows between two objects.

The series therefore specifies morphism class by class. Each kind carries a preservation law, the explicit conditions it must satisfy together with the rule under which two such morphisms compose, and a part states which morphism classes are in force whenever it uses the category. The strict reading of \(\mathrm{Phys}\) takes coarse-grainings, embeddings, and exact equivalences as its arrows; dualities are realized as functors between category presentations and limiting procedures as universal constructions in \(\mathfrak{C}\). That strict reading is the one in which the central conjecture is stated, and whether a single category carries all five kinds at once, or whether several related categories or a higher structure are required, is a research question of the program.

Continuum limits are the load-bearing instance. Lattice theorems in restricted regimes [24], [25], [26] do not by themselves close the continuum limit, and the series keeps the two apart.

Equivalence

Physical equivalence is a chosen relation on objects; the classification problem is the problem of classifying admissible representations up to that relation. Mathematical equivalence, isomorphism of the five-component tuples in the ambient category of each component, is strictly finer in general. Two mathematical descriptions may be physically equivalent without being mathematically isomorphic.

The series binds every Yoneda-based argument to one constraint, quoted from the founding document: “Yoneda does not, by itself, establish physical equivalence, empirical adequacy, or a classification theorem.” The Yoneda embedding sends an object to its functor of relationships, \[P \;\longmapsto\; \mathrm{Hom}_{\mathrm{Phys}}(-, P),\] and is faithful as mathematics. The physical interpretation of the resulting categorical structure is established separately.

A second constraint governs every equivalence used to identify systems. Any such equivalence must state which layer of structure it preserves. Equivalences that forget the monoidal or symmetric layer are provably insufficient: non-isomorphic finite groups carry equivalent representation categories as plain tensor categories [27]. A classification that discards the symmetric structure is therefore false. This requirement is the discipline the series applies to every equivalence, duality, and identification it records.

Families and invariants

The series organizes mathematical structures into ten candidate families, each a full subcategory singled out by its underlying structure. Symplectic and Poisson structures represent classical mechanics. Hilbert spaces and operator algebras represent quantum mechanics. Smooth manifolds and geometric structures represent relativity. Principal bundles and connections represent gauge theories. Probability and measure-theoretic structures represent statistical physics. Topological and higher-categorical structures represent topological field theories. Dynamical systems and operator semigroups represent dissipative and open systems. Tensor categories and compositional structures represent quantum information. Discrete combinatorial structures represent lattice and network models. Derived and homotopical structures represent higher gauge and derived field theories. The families are candidates for the generating collection of the central identity, and the identity asserts that they suffice.

For a theory \(T\) the series records an invariant tuple \[\mathcal{I}(T) = (\mathcal{S}_T, \mathcal{O}_T, \mathcal{D}_T, \mathcal{G}_T, \mathcal{R}_T)\] describing states, observables, dynamics, symmetries, and representations. A classifying invariant is one whose value determines the object up to the chosen equivalence. The series uses the following: a Dynkin or Satake diagram [2], a dominant integral weight [28], a dimension vector [1], a Murray-von Neumann type with Connes invariants [5], [6], a homotopy class of maps to a classifying space [3], [4], a fully dualizable object [29], [30], a holonomy group [22], a causal class [21], a set of entropy inequalities [31], and a Fisher metric up to constant [32].

The five components supply the bridge between the families. A cotangent bundle, a finite-dimensional operator algebra, and a principal bundle are instances of one tuple shape: each has a state space, an observable layer, a dynamics, a symmetry group, and a composition rule, and the invariant record \(\mathcal{I}(T)\) reads the same five slots in every case. The families differ in what fills the slots rather than in the slots themselves, and that common shape is what makes a cross-family classification problem well posed at all.

Admissibility and constructions

Definition 3 Definition 2 fix the domain and the generating operations of the structural classification conjecture. The admissible subcategory restricts the classification scope to mutually compatible components, and the construction set defines the inductive closure.

Definition 2 (Admissible constructions). The set \(\mathfrak{C}\) of admissible constructions consists of the tensor product, the direct sum, extension in the group, algebra, and field-theoretic senses, limits and colimits including the thermodynamic, infinite-volume, semiclassical, and continuum limits, and factorization of a representation into factors. These are operations on objects of \(\mathrm{Phys}\): each takes one or more systems and builds a new system, so each is a functor or a universal construction on the category rather than a component of any single system.

Definition 3 (Admissibility). An object \(P = (X, \mathcal{O}, \mathcal{D}, \mathcal{S}, \mathcal{C})\) is admissible when the five components are mutually consistent. Explicitly: the domain of \(\mathcal{D}\) is the state space \(X\) and its flow preserves the observable algebra \(\mathcal{O}\); the action of \(\mathcal{S}\) on \(X\) preserves \(\mathcal{D}\); and the internal composition rule \(\mathcal{C}\) is closed under composition of its own morphisms. The component \(\mathcal{C}\) is internal to a single system and records how that system composes with others; the construction set \(\mathfrak{C}\) of 2 is external and builds new systems from old ones. The admissible objects form the full subcategory \(\mathrm{Phys}_{\mathrm{adm}}\), which is closed under every construction of \(\mathfrak{C}\).

These two definitions fix the domain and the generating operation of the central conjecture. A part that uses \(\mathrm{Phys}_{\mathrm{adm}}\) works relative to the conditions of 3, and a part that decomposes a system uses the operations of 2.

The structural classification conjecture

Conjecture 1 (Structural classification of admissible physical systems). There exists a collection of fundamental structural families \(\mathfrak{F} = \{\mathfrak{F}_\alpha\}_{\alpha \in A}\) such that \[\mathrm{Phys}_{\mathrm{adm}} \simeq \langle \mathfrak{F}_\alpha \rangle_{\mathfrak{C}},\] the right side denoting closure of the families under the admissible constructions of 2.

The closure \(\langle \mathfrak{F} \rangle_{\mathfrak{C}}\) is the smallest full subcategory of \(\mathrm{Phys}\) that contains every \(\mathfrak{F}_\alpha\) and is closed under the constructions of 2. Its objects are exactly those obtained from the families by a finite iteration of those constructions, and no object outside that inductive closure is included. The conjecture is stated for the strict reading of \(\mathrm{Phys}\) described in 2.2, whose arrows are coarse-grainings, embeddings, and exact equivalences. In that reading \(\simeq\) denotes an equivalence of categories in the symmetric monoidal structure given by the tensor product construction, so the equivalence preserves the composition data of \(\mathcal{C}\).

The conjecture is the organizing statement of the series and enters every part as a declared hypothesis or as an object of study. It carries five proof obligations, each a theorem target.

Completeness requires that every admissible physical system is represented in the closure \(\langle \mathfrak{F} \rangle_{\mathfrak{C}}\). Soundness requires that every generated structure satisfies the admissibility conditions of 3. Equivalence requires that different representations of the same physical system are identified correctly, which is where the preserved-layer requirement [27] constrains the choice of \(\simeq\). Structural characterization requires that the fundamental families have precise mathematical definitions; the ten candidate families of 1 are named, and the admissibility conditions of 3 select among them. Decomposition requires that systems in the targeted classes can be constructed from the fundamental families by the operations of 2; restricted forms of this obligation are established for finite-dimensional \(C^*\)-algebras [1], amenable factors [5], [6], and superselection categories [10], [11].

Delivering the five obligations would complete the mathematical classification layer of the architecture. It would leave physical realization and empirical selection as separate problems.

The dependency graph

The series organizes as a directed acyclic graph rooted at the foundational ontology of Part I and terminating at the assembly sink of Part XII, drawn in 1. Every part has a directed path to Part XII, which takes registered inputs from all eleven earlier parts. No part bypasses the primitive ontology of Part I, and no part may cite a later one. A part may cite earlier parts for definitions and for registered results only, and no part may introduce an unproved result as a lemma.

Registered dependency records

Each permitted use of a registered result as an input to another part is recorded with its condition. A use is direct when the supplying result is a completed theorem in the literature, and a use is as hypothesis when the supplying statement is a conjecture, an open problem, or a conditional record. Classification theorems and parameterization schemes are different achievements across the surveyed sectors. The finite simple groups admit an exhaustive classification without a uniform parameterization, so practical identification runs through companion data of the ATLAS kind [34]. Classification invariants can also be purely global: Darboux normal form removes every local symplectic invariant beyond dimension [19], which forces symplectic classification into the global topological regime, the opposite of the Riemannian situation where curvature supplies rich local data.

Parameterized and normal-form results

Some sectors admit a parameterized answer rather than a finite list. Monotone metrics on quantum state spaces are classified by operator monotone functions through the Morozova-Chentsov construction [32]: the answer is a family indexed by a function, and no member of the family is distinguished among all monotone metrics. Every fusion category is the representation category of a weak Hopf algebra [39], and the additional structure that supplies canonical traces is the subject of a conjecture of Etingof, Nikshych, and Ostrik [40]. Every finite-dimensional algebra over an algebraically closed field is derived-tame or derived-wild [15], a dichotomy that sorts the field without classifying either side.

Other sectors carry a normal form rather than an isomorphism classification. Completely positive maps are classified up to minimal Stinespring dilation, unique up to unitary [41], and the Choi-Kraus form is a normal form whose Kraus rank is an invariant of the form [42]. The series treats this data as a normal form and not as an isomorphism classification of channels.

The regime taxonomy

Part XI sorts the surveyed domains by the kind of achievement each represents, and the sort is stable across the twelve parts: a domain keeps its regime when a different part studies it. Seven regimes cover the surveyed domains.

Complete finite invariant.

A theorem classifies the objects up to the chosen equivalence and the classifying invariant takes finitely many values or is a finite combinatorial datum. The Killing-Cartan classification and the dimension-vector classification live here.

Complete parameterized invariant.

A theorem classifies the objects and the invariant ranges over an explicitly described family. The Petz-Sudar classification of monotone metrics lives here.

Normal form.

A canonical form exists with a stated uniqueness, and the form is not an isomorphism classification of the objects. Stinespring dilation and the Choi-Kraus form live here.

Obstruction classification.

A theorem classifies the obstruction to a property rather than the objects. Anomaly classes live here.

Open regime.

Partial or parametric results exist and no completeness statement is available. Fusion categories and the Elliott program live here.

Claimed regime.

A result has been announced and its supporting artifacts are not publicly checkable. The four-dimensional existence announcement lives here.

Impossibility regime.

A theorem forbids classification of the stated kind. The McDuff obstruction, wildness, and four-manifold unrecognizability live here.

The taxonomy records boundaries as well as contents. Tame and wild separates the algorithmically tractable domains from the intractable ones [43], and dimension three separates from dimension four by the presence of a finite geometry table on one side and unrecognizable manifolds on the other [18], [36]. Those two boundaries are the ones the regime sort reproduces most sharply.

Sectors outside a finite list

Three sectors sit outside any finite classification in their stated generality. General type \(\mathrm{II}_1\) and type \(\mathrm{III}\) factors carry coarse Connes-spectrum strata and vary in their fundamental groups [23]. Extended topological quantum field theories are classified by fully dualizable objects [29], in a 2009 statement whose proof is an informal sketch [30], so the exact range of completed cases is a research question. The four-dimensional Yang-Mills existence problem is the subject of an announced result whose manuscript and formalization are withheld [44], [45], and the mass gap is a separate open problem [46]. The series records the existence claim and the mass gap separately and never merges them.

Equivalence and preserved structure

Every equivalence in the series names the structure it preserves, and the reason is a theorem rather than a preference.

The preserved-layer requirement

Two finite groups that are not isomorphic can carry equivalent representation categories as plain tensor categories [27]. The equivalence is monoidal and does not respect the commutativity constraint, so the symmetric braiding is the layer it fails to preserve. Any criterion of identification that discards the monoidal or symmetric layer therefore identifies distinct groups. The series adopts the resulting requirement: an equivalence used to identify systems must state which layer of structure it preserves, and an equivalence that forgets the symmetric layer is rejected as a criterion.

The requirement is constructive as well as negative. The Doplicher-Roberts equivalence takes a symmetric tensor \(*\)-category with conjugates, direct sums, subobjects, and \(\mathrm{End}(\mathbf{1})=\mathbb{C}\) to the finite-dimensional unitary representations of a compact supergroup, unique up to isomorphism [10], [11]. Deligne’s theorem takes a characteristic-zero symmetric tensor category of subexponential growth to a supergroup representation category [12]. Both statements preserve the symmetric structure and both state their hypotheses in full. Müger’s Galois theory for braided tensor categories recovers fixed-point data together with additional information that the braided structure alone does not supply [47].

Duality and the recovered data

Duality exchanges structure between differently presented theories, and each duality in the series states what the exchange recovers. Gelfand duality sends a commutative \(C^*\)-algebra to a compact Hausdorff space [8]. Stone duality sends a Boolean algebra to a zero-dimensional compact spectrum [9]. The classifying-space construction sends a principal \(G\)-bundle to a homotopy class of maps to \(BG\), with characteristic classes as pullbacks of universal classes [3], [4]. The Yoneda embedding sends an object to its functor of relationships and is faithful as mathematics; the constraint that it does not by itself establish physical equivalence, empirical adequacy, or a classification theorem governs every use of it in the series.

A duality is not automatically a physical equivalence. The series keeps the two apart: an identification of representations is a mathematical statement, and an identification of physical content requires its own definition and justification. The proposed gravity-information correspondence is a case in point. Ryu-Takayanagi relations are verified exactly in anti-de Sitter space in three dimensions with a two-dimensional conformal field theory dual [48], entanglement-wedge reconstruction holds as a physical dictionary result under the semiclassical AdS/CFT assumptions [49], and tensor-network models are toys rather than evidence for the duality itself [50]. The series states each of these at the strength the source supports.

Observation and distinguishability

Observation functors formalize what an empirical procedure can separate. Applied to a pair of systems they induce an equivalence relation: systems are observationally equivalent relative to the procedure when no observation in its range separates them. The induced relation is coarse when the functor forgets detail and fine when it resolves it, and it behaves well under composition of procedures.

The metric content of distinguishability diverges between the classical and quantum cases. On a classical probability simplex the Fisher information metric is the unique Riemannian metric monotone under stochastic transitions up to a positive constant [32]. On quantum state spaces the monotone metrics form a family parameterized by operator monotone functions, and no member of that family is distinguished [32]. The series treats the classical uniqueness as the anchor and the quantum parameterization as the reality, and it does not extend uniqueness across the gap between them.

Mathematical isomorphism and observational indistinguishability are different relations. A mathematical classification would not by itself select a theory empirically, and several mathematically distinct theories produce identical predictions in an experimentally accessible regime. The three-layer architecture of the assembly keeps mathematical classification, physical realization, and empirical selection as separate maps, and no part of the series collapses them.

Reconstruction theorems fail when the probe category loses the hypotheses that carry the determination. The Doplicher-Roberts recovery of a compact supergroup collapses without the symmetric braiding, as non-isomorphic groups with equivalent plain tensor categories show [27]. Deligne’s classification fails without the subexponential growth condition on categorical dimensions [12]. Gelfand duality ceases to recover a topological spectrum once commutativity is dropped [8]. Each hypothesis is therefore a boundary of the theorem rather than a technical convenience.

Whether the 2024 to 2026 supermap-representability line [51], [52] unifies with the Gelfand, Stone, and Naimark pattern into a single relational principle is a research question. The series records the proved representability results at their stated strength and leaves the unification open.

Obstruction theorems

Obstruction theorems prove that no classification of a stated kind can exist in a stated generality. They are content of the series, and they are what a periodic table must mark in the places where no table exists. The register appears in 7.

Dimension three and dimension four

The clearest scope contrast in the series is dimensional. Closed three-manifolds decompose into exactly eight Thurston geometries [36], [37], a finite table with a complete decomposition theorem behind it. In dimension four the model geometries are countably infinite in one family [36], only restricted classes decompose, and homeomorphism of a specific four-manifold is algorithmically unrecognizable [18]. Finite classification tables exist in tame regimes and stop at the dimension where unrecognizability begins. The series states the contrast as a scope boundary and builds its regime taxonomy around it.

Scope effects

Each obstruction removes a possibility rather than adding a difficulty. The McDuff construction [13] leaves the amenable classification intact and closes the non-amenable case. The isocategorical construction [27] leaves the symmetric theory intact and closes the plain tensor case. The Adyan-Rabin results [14] close uniform recognition while leaving individual presentations decidable. The wildness theorems [15], [16], [17] close finite-variety parameterization while leaving the tame side open to classification. Reading the register this way is what allows a classification program to state restricted-class theorems with explicit scope instead of promising a single global table.

Worked examples

Three systems trace the shared framework end to end. Each is an object of the category, each carries an invariant record, and each sits in a regime of the taxonomy.

A finite-dimensional quantum system

Take a finite-dimensional quantum system whose observable algebra is a unital finite-dimensional complex \(C^*\)-algebra. The five components of the representation are immediate: the state space is the set of density operators on the underlying Hilbert space, the observable layer is the algebra \(A\), the dynamics is a one-parameter group of \(*\)-automorphisms, the symmetry data is the group of automorphisms that preserve the dynamics, and the composition rule is the minimal tensor product of algebras.

The algebra decomposes as \[A \cong \bigoplus_{i=1}^{k} M_{n_i}(\mathbb{C}),\] and the multiset of matrix sizes is the dimension vector. Two such algebras are \(*\)-isomorphic exactly when their dimension vectors agree [1]. The dimension vector is therefore a classifying invariant of regime one, and it is finite data. Irreducibility is relative to the composition rule: the simple blocks \(M_{n_i}(\mathbb{C})\) are the irreducible objects under direct sum, and the decomposition into blocks is unique up to order.

Morphisms between such systems are \(*\)-homomorphisms, and the equivalence that identifies representations is \(*\)-isomorphism, which preserves the full algebra structure. Channels between systems are completely positive maps; Stinespring dilation classifies them up to minimal dilation and unitary freedom [41], and the Kraus rank is an invariant of the normal form [42]. The example therefore exercises the invariant discipline, the decomposition discipline, and the normal-form regime at once.

A classical mechanical system

Take a cotangent bundle \(T^*Q\) with its canonical symplectic form. The state space is the symplectic manifold, the observable layer is the algebra of smooth functions with the Poisson bracket, the dynamics is a Hamiltonian vector field, the symmetry data is the group of canonical transformations preserving the Hamiltonian, and the composition rule is the product of symplectic manifolds.

Darboux normal form shows that all symplectic manifolds of the same dimension are locally symplectomorphic [19]. Local classification content on the symplectic side is therefore empty, and every invariant is global: the topology of the symplectic form, the Chern classes of the prequantum data, and the coadjoint orbits in the dual of a Lie algebra [20]. The example shows why the local and global regimes must be separated in the taxonomy. A Poisson manifold splits near a point of rank \(2k\) as a symplectic factor times a transverse Poisson factor [20], which is the local model the classification uses.

For a statistical mechanical system the state space is a probability simplex and the invariant data is the Fisher metric, unique up to constant among metrics monotone under stochastic maps [32]. The classical uniqueness is the anchor for the observational layer, and the quantum analogue is parameterized rather than unique.

A gauge theory

Take a principal \(G\)-bundle \(P\) over a base space \(X\) with a connection. The state space is the space of connections modulo gauge, the observable layer is the algebra of gauge-invariant functions, the dynamics is the Yang-Mills or Chern-Simons flow, the symmetry data is the gauge group, and the composition rule is the fiber product of bundles.

Isomorphism classes of principal \(G\)-bundles over \(X\) are homotopy classes of maps from \(X\) to the classifying space \(BG\) [3], [4], and the characteristic classes are pullbacks of universal classes in the cohomology of \(BG\). The classification is therefore complete and homotopy-theoretic. Anomalies are classified as obstruction classes in the Chevalley-Eilenberg cohomology of the gauge algebra [54]: quantization exists exactly when the relevant classes vanish. Superselection sectors of the associated quantum field theory form a symmetric tensor \(*\)-category, and the Doplicher-Roberts theorem recovers the compact gauge group from that category under its stated hypotheses [10], [11].

The example is the one place in the series where the classification layer, the obstruction layer, and the reconstruction layer all appear in a single system. It also shows the load-bearing character of the preserved-layer requirement: the recovery of the gauge group uses the symmetric structure of the sector category, and discarding that structure admits the isocategorical counterexamples [27].

Emergent structure

The twelve parts jointly establish several findings that no single part establishes alone. They are the emergent content of the composition.

A scope law holds across the aggregate. Complete invariants concentrate where the invariant is finite or explicitly parameterized, and beyond that boundary the series records either a parameterized family or an obstruction theorem. The law is visible only in the aggregate: individual parts classify their own sectors, and the obstruction register appears as a separate part, while the correspondence between the two is a property of the whole.

An asymmetry separates reconstruction from classification. Reconstruction theorems recur across the series, each recovering an object from probes, and each under hypotheses that cannot be dropped. Complete classifications recur much less often and never outside finite or parametric regimes. The asymmetry is a structural feature of the subject matter rather than an artifact of the survey: the probe categories that reconstruction uses are richer invariants than the finite data that classification needs.

A preserved-layer law governs the equivalence content. Every equivalence, duality, and identification the series records either states the structure it preserves or records the failure that follows from not stating it. The negative theorem of Etingof and Gelaki [27] is the reason, and the discipline is uniform across all twelve parts.

A layer separation holds in the architecture. Mathematical classification, physical realization, and empirical selection are three maps with different domains and codomains. A complete mathematical classification would leave realization and selection unconstrained, because the latter two apply maps that classification does not compute. The separation is stated in the foundational part, instantiated in the observational-equivalence part, and preserved in the assembly part.

These findings fix the scope of any answer to the program’s question. Structural classification of physical systems is realizable as restricted-class classification theorems stated with their invariants and explicit scope, bounded by obstruction theorems that mark where no table exists. Complete classifications succeed where the classifying data reduce to finite invariants or tame parameterizations. Algorithmic undecidability in four-manifold topology and wild representation type rule out a single global table.

Contrasts the series maintains

Several pairs of results pull against each other, and the series keeps each pair apart rather than resolving it prematurely.

Classification and reconstruction are different achievements. Classification is sharp where the invariant is finite or parametric, and reconstruction routinely recovers an object from probes [7], [8], [9], [10], [12]. The parts that study the two do not merge them.

Local and global classification content differ by subject. Darboux normal form empties the local symplectic invariant theory [19], and Riemannian curvature supplies rich local data. Symplectic classification is therefore global, and the series states the difference as a property of the subject.

Classical uniqueness and quantum parameterization do not match. The Fisher metric is unique up to constant in the classical case [32], and the quantum monotone metrics form a family indexed by operator monotone functions. No part of the series extends the classical uniqueness across that gap.

Relational determination and physical equivalence do not coincide. The Yoneda embedding determines an object by its relationships, and the qualification attached to every use of it records that physical equivalence, empirical adequacy, and classification do not follow from that determination alone.

Announcement and evidence are different things. The constructive quantum field theory frontier carries several announced results [44], [45], and the series records each at the strength of its publicly checkable evidence, keeping existence and the mass gap separate [46].

Mathematical consistency and empirical selection are different layers. A consistency proof settles the first and leaves the second open, because the empirical layer applies an observation map that mathematical classification does not compute.

Cross-volume consistency

The twelve parts maintain uniform definition identifiers, respect the directed dependency discipline, and enforce the preserved-layer constraint throughout. The corpus satisfies the following eight structural properties.

Ontology identity.

The forty-two definition identifiers are used with one meaning across all twelve parts, and later parts extend the list only by appending new identifiers.

Definitional root.

Every part works in the ontology of Part I, and no part redefines an object that Part I owns.

Dependency discipline.

Every cross-part use of a registered result is a permitted edge of 1, and unproven statements enter only as declared hypotheses or as objects of study.

Yoneda qualification.

The constraint on Yoneda-based arguments appears in the same wording in every part that uses Yoneda methods.

Preserved-layer requirement.

Every equivalence and duality recorded across the series names the structure it preserves or records the failure of recovery.

Notation.

The five-component tuple, the invariant record, and the closure notation are used in one form throughout.

Existence and mass gap.

The announced four-dimensional existence result and the mass gap problem are recorded as separate items in every part that mentions them.

Entropy cone range.

The holographic entropy cone is completely characterized for five boundary regions and fewer [31], [38], and the open range begins at six regions. This entry is the correction.

Four of these findings are load-bearing for the series as a whole. The definitional root is what makes the twelve parts one program rather than twelve adjacent papers: a definition introduced in Part I has one meaning everywhere. The dependency discipline is what makes the composition honest: a part that needs an unproven statement declares it as a hypothesis instead of using it as a lemma. The preserved-layer requirement is what makes the equivalence content of the series correct rather than merely plausible: the counterexample of Etingof and Gelaki [27] rules out the weaker discipline by theorem. The separation of existence from mass gap is what keeps the constructive quantum field theory material accurate [46], [44], [45].

The corrected entry concerns the holographic entropy cone. The cone for five boundary regions is completely characterized: the cone defined by all proven inequalities admits graph models for every extreme ray orbit, which settles the existence of further five-party inequalities in the negative [38]. The cone is open from six regions onward [31], and the periodic table of 5 records the corrected range.

Conclusion

The structural picture of the series is a classification program with a stated scope. One ontology of forty-two definitions organizes the objects, morphisms, equivalences, families, and invariants. One dependency graph composes the twelve parts. One equivalence requirement forces every identification to name the structure it preserves. Around the central conjecture, the mathematical content sorts into regimes with complete invariants, regimes with parameterized or normal-form answers, and regimes where obstruction theorems prove that no classification of the stated kind exists.

The regime structure is the durable finding. Complete invariants appear where the invariant is finite or explicitly parameterized. Reconstruction from probes is better developed than classification and carries hypotheses that cannot be dropped. Obstruction theorems mark a sharp scope boundary: dimension three admits a finite table of geometries and dimension four contains unrecognizable manifolds, the amenable factors are classified and the general type \(\mathrm{II}_1\) factors are not, the symmetric tensor categories of subexponential growth are classified and the plain tensor categories do not determine their groups.

The central conjecture states the program’s theorem target and carries the five obligations that would discharge it. Restricted forms of the decomposition obligation are established in the sectors where complete invariants exist. The remaining obligations are the research program.
