# F1: CFSG + classical classification theorems structure

## Findings

### [1] The CFSG statement has the "families + sporadics" architecture: every finite simple group is cyclic of prime order, alternating of degree >= 5, one of 16 infinite families of Lie type (18 infinite families total), or one of 26 sporadic groups (27 if the Tits group is counted as sporadic).
- quote: "Every finite simple group is, up to isomorphism, one of the following groups: a member of 18 specific infinite families of simple groups, namely, cyclic groups of prime order, alternating groups of degree at least 5, 16 other infinite families known as the simple groups of Lie type, or one of 26 specific groups called the 'sporadic groups'."
- url: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Classification_of_finite_simple_groups
- source_type: secondary
- published: unknown (page revision 2026)
- confidence: high

### [2] The CFSG proof spans tens of thousands of pages in several hundred journal articles by about 100 authors, published mostly 1955-2004; Gorenstein's 1983 completion announcement was premature, and the completed proof was announced by Aschbacher (2004) only after Aschbacher and Smith published a 1221-page quasithin case.
- quote: "The proof consists of tens of thousands of pages in several hundred journal articles written by about 100 authors, published mostly between 1955 and 2004. ... The completed proof of the classification was announced by Aschbacher (2004) after Aschbacher and Smith published a 1221-page proof for the missing quasithin case."
- url: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Classification_of_finite_simple_groups
- source_type: secondary
- published: unknown (page revision 2026)
- confidence: high

### [3] CFSG's proof architecture is a case division by local 2-structure: small 2-rank handled first (via character theory), then groups split into "component type" and "characteristic 2 type" (balance theorem + connected Sylow 2-subgroups for sectional 2-rank >= 5), with classification proceeding through centralizers of involutions.
- quote: "All groups not of small 2-rank can be split into two major classes: groups of component type and groups of characteristic 2 type. ... A group is said to be of component type if for some centralizer C of an involution, C/O(C) has a component."
- url: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Classification_of_finite_simple_groups
- source_type: secondary
- published: unknown (page revision 2026)
- confidence: high

### [4] Gorenstein's 1972 program for completing CFSG was a 16-step plan (low 2-rank, standard form in odd characteristic, thin and quasithin groups, signalizer functors, characteristic 2 type, trichotomy, etc.), and the final classification followed this outline closely.
- quote: "In 1972 Gorenstein (1979, Appendix) announced a program for completing the classification of finite simple groups, consisting of the following 16 steps: ... Quasithin groups. ... This was completed by Aschbacher and Smith in 2004."
- url: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Classification_of_finite_simple_groups
- source_type: secondary
- published: unknown (page revision 2026)
- confidence: high

### [5] The second-generation (GLS "revisionist") proof: 10 volumes published as of 2023 (Gorenstein–Lyons–Solomon 1994–2023), estimated at roughly 5,000 pages when complete; it is shorter because the final statement is known, letting weaker-but-sufficient techniques and a non-redundant case division replace first-generation generality.
- quote: "As of 2023, ten volumes of the second generation proof have been published ... It is estimated that the new proof will eventually fill approximately 5,000 pages. ... The most important thing is that the correct, final statement of the theorem is now known."
- url: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Classification_of_finite_simple_groups
- source_type: secondary
- published: unknown (page revision 2026)
- confidence: high

### [6] Gorenstein explicitly contrasted CFSG with the compact Lie group classification: unlike the Dynkin-diagram parameterization, "no one has yet found a clean uniform description of the finite simple groups", and Atiyah-style geometric-object strategies fail because usable geometric structure (e.g. BN-pairs) only appears at the very end of the analysis.
- quote: "So far no one has yet found a clean uniform description of the finite simple groups similar to the parameterization of the compact Lie groups by Dynkin diagrams. ... The problem is that no one has been able to suggest an easy way to find such a geometric structure associated with a simple group."
- url: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Classification_of_finite_simple_groups
- source_type: secondary
- published: unknown (page revision 2026)
- confidence: high

### [7] The ATLAS of Finite Groups (Conway, Curtis, Norton, Parker, Wilson; Oxford UP, December 1985, 294 pp., ISBN 978-0-19-853199-9) lists 93 finite simple groups with the invariants practitioners use for identification: order, Schur multiplier, outer automorphism group, presentations, maximal subgroups, and character tables with power maps; it is continued as the electronic ATLAS of Finite Group Representations (V2.0 1999, V3 at brauer.maths.qmul.ac.uk).
- quote: "It lists basic information about 93 finite simple groups. ... The information provided is generally a group's order, Schur multiplier, outer automorphism group, various constructions ..., conjugacy classes of maximal subgroups, and, most importantly, character tables (including power maps on the conjugacy classes)."
- url: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ATLAS_of_Finite_Groups
- source_type: secondary
- published: unknown (page revision 2026-02-25)
- confidence: high

### [8] The Killing–Cartan classification of simple Lie groups is layered: complex semisimple Lie algebras are classified by Dynkin diagrams (4 infinite series A_r, B_r, C_r, D_r plus 5 exceptional diagrams G2, F4, E6, E7, E8); real simple algebras are classified by real forms via Satake diagrams (each complex algebra has always at least a split and a compact form; real forms correspond to automorphism classes of order at most 2); centerless groups follow from algebras, and all others are central quotients of universal covers.
- quote: "The classification is usually stated in several steps, namely: Classification of simple complex Lie algebras ... by Dynkin diagrams. Classification of simple real Lie algebras ... classified by additional decorations of its Dynkin diagram called Satake diagrams ... There are always at least 2 such forms: a split form and a compact form ... The different real forms correspond to the classes of automorphisms of order at most 2 of the complex Lie algebra."
- url: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simple_Lie_group
- source_type: secondary
- published: unknown (page revision 2025)
- confidence: high

### [9] The theorem of the highest weight classifies finite-dimensional irreducible representations of a complex semisimple Lie algebra by dominant integral weights: a bijection lambda -> [V^lambda], proven in three parts (unique highest weight is dominant integral; same highest weight implies isomorphic; existence for every dominant integral lambda) — the construction/existence part is the hardest; originally Cartan (1913), compact-group version Weyl.
- quote: "The theorem states that there is a bijection λ ↦ [V^λ] from the set of 'dominant integral elements' to the set of equivalence classes of irreducible representations of g or K. ... The most difficult part is the last one; the construction of a finite-dimensional irreducible representation with a prescribed highest weight."
- url: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theorem_of_the_highest_weight
- source_type: secondary
- published: unknown (page revision 2026-05-17)
- confidence: high

### [10] Darboux's theorem (1882, "Sur le problème de Pfaff") gives a complete local normal form for symplectic structures: any two symplectic manifolds of the same dimension are locally symplectomorphic, so symplectic geometry has NO local invariants — the exact opposite of Riemannian geometry, where curvature obstructs local normal form away from the point; the modern proof is Moser's trick, and Weinstein (1971) strengthened it to neighborhoods of submanifolds.
- quote: "Darboux's theorem for symplectic manifolds implies that there are no local invariants in symplectic geometry: a Darboux basis can always be taken, valid near any given point. This is in marked contrast to the situation in Riemannian geometry where the curvature is a local invariant."
- url: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Darboux%27s_theorem
- source_type: secondary
- published: unknown (page revision 2026-08-11)
- confidence: high

### [11] For Poisson geometry the local model is the Weinstein splitting theorem (Darboux–Weinstein): near a point of rank 2k a Poisson manifold splits as a symplectic factor times a transverse Poisson structure vanishing at the point (Darboux is the nondegenerate case); globally a Poisson manifold foliates into symplectic leaves, and for the Lie–Poisson (KKS / Kirillov–Kostant–Souriau) structure on g* the leaves are exactly the coadjoint orbits.
- quote: "any Poisson manifold (M^n, π) splits locally around a point x0 ∈ M as the product of a symplectic manifold (S^{2k}, ω) and a transverse Poisson submanifold (T^{n−2k}, π_T) vanishing at x0. ... The symplectic leaves of the Lie-Poisson structure on g* are the orbits of the coadjoint action of G on g*."
- url: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poisson_manifold
- source_type: secondary
- published: 2024 (article peer-reviewed in WikiJournal of Science 7(1):6, doi:10.15347/wjs/2024.006; Poisson structures introduced by Lichnerowicz 1977, foundational Weinstein 1983)
- confidence: high
