# Why This May Be the Right Moment to Classify Mathematical Physics

The development you describe is potentially significant for our **Classification of Mathematical Representations of Physical Systems (CMRPS)** program.

The reason is not simply that AI may have helped solve a major mathematical physics problem. It is that **constructive quantum field theory, formal verification, category theory, and AI-assisted mathematical research are beginning to converge**.

That convergence could change what is practically achievable in foundational physics.

## 1. From describing physical theories to proving their existence

Historically, theoretical physics has often proceeded by constructing mathematical models that successfully predict observations, even when their complete mathematical foundations remain unresolved.

Four-dimensional interacting quantum field theories are a particularly important example.

Physicists use quantum chromodynamics with extraordinary predictive success, but establishing its rigorous nonperturbative mathematical existence is a different problem.

The result you quoted claims progress on precisely this distinction.

| Physical theory | Mathematical significance |
|---|---|
| Pure Yang–Mills | Construction of a four-dimensional interacting non-Abelian gauge theory |
| QCD | Construction incorporating gauge fields and interacting fermionic matter |
| Grand unified completion | Construction of a theory containing the Standard Model's gauge and fermion content |
| General relativity | Existing rigorous classical geometric framework, with quantum gravity unresolved |

If the claimed proofs withstand independent mathematical review, they would represent an important advance in constructive quantum field theory.

However, the existence claims should be distinguished from a complete solution of the Clay Yang–Mills problem, which also requires establishing a positive mass gap. Likewise, constructing a grand unified completion does not establish that it is the empirically correct theory of nature.

## 2. Why this changes our classification strategy

Consider the analogy with finite simple groups.

The classification theorem does not merely list groups. It establishes that every finite simple group belongs to the classified families.

We want to investigate whether a similar mathematical completeness principle can be established for physical theories.

The existence of rigorously constructed interacting quantum field theories would provide additional mathematical objects that our classification framework must accommodate.

We could investigate a hierarchy:

\[
\boxed{
\text{Mathematical Structures}
\longrightarrow
\text{Admissible Physical Theories}
\longrightarrow
\text{Rigorous Constructions}
\longrightarrow
\text{Empirical Realizations}
}
\]

Each transition requires different theorems.

For example, proving that a mathematical structure exists does not automatically establish that it describes a physical system. Nor does constructing one model establish that every model belongs to a known family.

**Our classification program would attempt to make these distinctions mathematically precise.**

## 3. The opportunity for YonedaAI

Our existing research directions are unusually relevant to the mathematical questions involved.

The Yoneda Constraint investigates relational characterization.

Condensed Representation Theory investigates representations, sheaves, and structural relationships.

Information Geometry Correspondence investigates observational equivalence.

Emergent Spacetime investigates reconstruction of geometry.

Gravity–Information–Geometry investigates the relationship between informational structures and gravitational dynamics.

Principia Physica seeks to integrate these mathematical foundations.

Together, they suggest an architecture in which mathematical theories are classified not merely by their equations but by their structures, representations, and relationships.

One possible formalization is to associate a physical theory \(T\) with a structured collection of invariants:

\[
\mathcal I(T)=
\left(
\mathcal S_T,
\mathcal O_T,
\mathcal D_T,
\mathcal G_T,
\mathcal R_T
\right)
\]

where the components describe states, observables, dynamics, symmetries, and representations.

We would then investigate which invariants are sufficient to distinguish theories up to a specified equivalence.

This is where category theory and representability become especially useful.

## 4. Formal verification makes the program more realistic

One of the greatest obstacles to a classification theorem of this scale is the enormous number of interacting mathematical definitions, lemmas, and constructions.

Formal proof assistants such as Lean offer a way to make those dependencies explicit and machine-checkable.

AI-assisted mathematical research may accelerate the construction of definitions, identification of candidate theorems, proof exploration, and formalization.

That suggests a practical research architecture:

\[
\boxed{
\text{Classification}
+
\text{AI-Assisted Discovery}
+
\text{Formal Verification}
}
\]

The goal would be to build a growing, formally verified mathematical library in which every new physical structure can be connected to established structures.

This would also expose gaps in our proposed universal framework.

## 5. What we should do first

I would prioritize a rigorous classification of a restricted class of mathematical physical systems rather than immediately attempting the universal theorem.

Finite-dimensional quantum systems, finite groupoid models, and selected classes of topological quantum field theories offer useful starting points because substantial classification machinery already exists.

We could then investigate how these classes relate to more difficult structures, including four-dimensional gauge theories.

The resulting program would have three successive goals:

**First:** Establish a mathematically precise taxonomy of admissible structures.

**Second:** Prove reconstruction and equivalence theorems connecting those structures.

**Third:** Investigate whether the established families generate a broader class of physical theories.

The third goal is the analogue of the completeness theorem in the classification of finite simple groups.

## 6. The larger significance

There is a historical distinction worth emphasizing.

The classification of finite simple groups was possible because mathematicians had developed sufficiently precise definitions, powerful structural theorems, and methods for organizing a vast body of proofs.

Our proposed classification of mathematical physics faces a similar organizational challenge, but with an additional complication: we do not yet know whether an exhaustive classification of all admissible physical theories exists in a useful mathematical sense.

That uncertainty should become part of the research itself.

We should be prepared to prove either that a specified class admits a complete classification or that certain natural classification objectives are impossible without additional restrictions.

Both would be meaningful mathematical results.

### My assessment

**The timing is compelling because the tools for undertaking the research are becoming substantially more powerful, while foundational questions about the mathematical organization of physical theories remain open.**

If the reported constructive QFT results are independently validated, they would strengthen this opportunity by adding rigorously established four-dimensional interacting theories to the mathematical landscape.

But we do not need to wait for that validation to begin.

The mathematical foundations of the classification program can be developed now.

I would make this a central objective of *Principia Physica*:

> **To identify, classify, and formally verify the fundamental mathematical structures capable of representing physical systems, and to determine whether all admissible physical theories can be reconstructed from a complete set of structural families.**

That is a sufficiently precise ambition to organize a substantial, long-term mathematical research program—and one whose individual components can produce valuable results even if a universal classification theorem ultimately proves unattainable.
