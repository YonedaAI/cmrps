Abstract

This paper assembles the registered results of the Classification of Mathematical Representations of Physical Systems into the three-layer architecture of Principia Physica: mathematical classification, physical realization, and empirical selection. The assembly keeps the three layers apart and separates proved statements from conjectural ones. Twenty background classification theorems and seven obstruction theorems are organized into seven classification regimes; a finite case analysis of the twenty-eight domains of the program comparison spine, with one boundary case, shows that fifteen carry a classification theorem with a finite or explicitly parameterized invariant, one is complete but unparameterized, one is a complete obstruction classification, seven remain open or claimed, three are impossible in the stated generality, one is undecidable, and one is decidable but unclassified. Eight reconstruction theorems recover objects from probes, each under hypotheses that cannot be dropped. The empirical layer admits no unique monotone metric in the quantum case, and no record validates the physical layer: the announced four-dimensional existence result carries the status existence claimed; manuscript and formalization withheld; verification pending, and the mass gap problem remains open. The structural classification statement enters only as a declared hypothesis, and a conditional reduction records what its five proof obligations would deliver. No record in the series carries a completed computer-checked proof.

Introduction

A unified structural theory of mathematical physics would answer three questions at once: which mathematical structures can represent physical systems, how those structures realize concrete physical theories, and how observation selects among the theories that survive. The Classification of Mathematical Representations of Physical Systems (CMRPS) program treats the three questions as three layers of a single architecture and studies them separately. This paper is the assembly layer of that program. It takes the registered results of the eleven preceding volumes and states, at exactly the strength the evidence supports, what a unified structural theory would require. It proves no new classification theorem; its formal statements are deductions about the assembled evidence and about what the central conjecture would deliver. The program documents and the eleven preceding volumes are works of this series and are available from the program site https://yonedalabs.ai. They organize citations and claim statuses and add no authority of their own: every mathematical statement used below is cited to the external literature, with a resolvable primary source for each citation.

The assembly reveals that empirical validation remains unachieved. The program has established mathematical formulations and rigorous theorems; it has not established physical validation of the architecture itself. Three levels of mathematical achievement separate formulation, rigorous theorem, and physical validation, and the evidence base reaches the second level only. Every claim below is calibrated to that ladder.

The central object is the identity \[\mathrm{Phys}_{\mathrm{adm}} \simeq \langle \mathfrak{F}_\alpha \rangle_{\mathfrak{C}},\] which asserts that every admissible physical system is generated from a collection of fundamental structural families under admissible constructions. The identity is a conjecture and is never used here as a lemma. The results assembled around it are the classification regimes where theorems exist, the obstruction theorems that forbid classification elsewhere, the reconstruction theorems that recover objects from probes, and the empirical constraints that no mathematical classification can discharge.

This assembly establishes four results. Twenty-eight mathematical domains fall into six realized classification regimes, with complete invariants appearing only where the invariant is finite or explicitly parameterized. Seven impossibility theorems show that wild representation categories, non-amenable factors, and generic four-manifolds admit no uniform classification. Every reconstruction theorem on record requires its full algebraic hypotheses, and dropping the symmetric or monoidal structure admits non-isomorphic counterexamples. Resolving the structural classification conjecture would complete the mathematical classification alone, leaving physical realization and empirical selection unconstrained.

A mathematical classification is never stated as a physical realization, and neither is ever stated as empirical selection. The strongest defensible claim on record is the founding program’s own calibration: the architecture is precise enough to organize a substantial, long-term mathematical research program, and its individual components produce valuable results even if a universal classification theorem ultimately proves unattainable.

Definitions

Stipulated notions are marked as such; established mathematics is cited to primary sources.

Definition 1 (Representation of a physical system). A representation of a physical system is a tuple \[P = (X, \mathcal{O}, \mathcal{D}, \mathcal{S}, \mathcal{C}),\] comprising a state space or geometric structure \(X\), an observable algebra or collection \(\mathcal{O}\), dynamical laws \(\mathcal{D}\), symmetry and constraint data \(\mathcal{S}\), and composition rules \(\mathcal{C}\). The five components need not be independent.

1 is a stipulative schema, not a theorem. Instances of \(X\) range over symplectic manifolds [6], [7], Hilbert spaces [9], probability spaces, smooth manifolds [46], [47], and lattice configurations. The intended ambient structure \(\mathrm{Phys}\) takes these representations as objects and admissible relationships as morphisms. Coarse-graining, equivalences, embeddings, dualities, and limiting procedures are candidate morphism kinds, and their preservation laws and composition rules are not fixed. Until they are fixed, \(\mathrm{Phys}\) is a schema rather than a category, and every construction below is read at the level of object classes: no categorical subcategory, limit, or colimit construction is invoked.

Definition 2 (Equivalences). Mathematical equivalence is isomorphism of the tuples of 1 in the ambient category of each component. Physical equivalence \(\simeq\) is a chosen equivalence relation on the objects of \(\mathrm{Phys}\); the classification problem is \(\mathrm{Classify}(\mathrm{Ob}(\mathrm{Phys})/\simeq)\).

Physical equivalence is stipulative and the choice is open. Two mathematical descriptions may be physically equivalent without being mathematically isomorphic. Any equivalence used to identify systems must state which layer of structure it preserves; equivalences that forget the monoidal or symmetric layer are provably insufficient [12].

Definition 3 (Admissible systems and family closure). An admissible physical system is an object of the schema of 1 satisfying explicitly stated consistency conditions; \(\mathrm{Phys}_{\mathrm{adm}}\) denotes the class of such objects. A structural family collection \(\mathfrak{F} = \{\mathfrak{F}_\alpha\}_{\alpha \in A}\) is a collection of classes of objects singled out by their underlying mathematical structure, and \(\langle \mathfrak{F} \rangle_{\mathfrak{C}}\) denotes the smallest class of objects containing \(\mathfrak{F}\) and closed under a set of admissible constructions \(\mathfrak{C}\): tensor product, direct sum, extension, limits and colimits, and factorization.

The admissibility conditions are the first deliverable of the foundations volume and remain unfixed. Every statement quantified over \(\mathrm{Phys}_{\mathrm{adm}}\) is therefore conditional on the chosen conditions. The ten candidate structural families are named in the shared ontology and range from symplectic and Poisson structures through operator algebras, manifolds, bundles, probability, higher categories, dynamical systems, tensor categories, combinatorial structures, and derived structures. They are candidate organizing families, and some may later be unified.

Definition 4 (Irreducibility and decomposition). An object \(P\) is irreducible relative to a specified composition operation when it admits no nontrivial decomposition under that operation. A decomposition is an expression \(P \simeq \mathcal{F}(I_1, \ldots, I_n)\) with each \(I_i\) irreducible and \(\mathcal{F}\) an admissible construction. Irreducibility is always relative to the composition data \(\mathcal{C}\) and to the decomposition notion in force.

Interacting physical systems need not admit unique decompositions. Entanglement, emergence, gauge redundancy, and nontrivial interactions all complicate the notion, so existence-and-uniqueness theorems belong only to restricted subclasses.

Definition 5 (The three layers). The Principia architecture consists of three layers. The mathematical layer is \(\mathcal{M} = \mathrm{Classify}(\text{admissible structures})\). The realization layer is a map \(\mathcal{R} : \mathcal{M} \to \mathcal{P}\) into physical theories. The selection layer is a map \(\mathcal{E} : \mathcal{P} \to \mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{obs}}\) into observational constraints or predictions. The object \(\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{obs}}\) records observational data; the map \(\mathcal{E}\) does not assert that every model maps to a single observational object.

Definition 6 (Evidence grades). A statement carries grade 1 when it is a mathematical formulation, grade 2 when a rigorous proof exists in the literature, and grade 3 when the statement has achieved physical validation, meaning that its physical realization and its empirical selection have been established for the systems it describes.

Definition 7 (Classification regimes). A domain of mathematical structures is assigned to exactly one of seven regimes. Regime 1 is complete and parameterized: a classification theorem exists and a finite or explicitly described parameter class determines the object. Parameter classes range from finite data such as dimension vectors to infinite function classes such as operator monotone functions, and the classification is a bijection with the parameter class in either case. Regime 2 is complete but unparameterized: a classification theorem exists and no uniform parameterization is known. Regime 3 is complete as obstruction: the classification content is the obstruction itself. Regime 4 is open: partial results, normal forms, or claims only. Regime 5 is impossible in the stated generality: a theorem forbids the classification. Regime 6 is undecidable: no algorithm can carry the classification. Regime 7 is decidable but unclassified.

Remark 8. The regime assignment is an organizing device for the evidence base. It does not assert that the regimes are mathematically exhaustive of all possible classification situations. The unknot recognition problem, in \(\mathrm{NP}\) and co-\(\mathrm{NP}\) with the complexity-class membership open [57], is the registered instance of regime 7, and 3 carries it as a boundary case beyond the twenty-eight spine domains.

Definition 9 (Structural invariant record). For a theory \(T\), the structural invariant record is the tuple \[\mathcal{I}(T) = (\mathcal{S}_T, \mathcal{O}_T, \mathcal{D}_T, \mathcal{G}_T, \mathcal{R}_T),\] describing states, observables, dynamics, symmetries, and representations. The classification question is which of these invariants suffice to distinguish theories up to the chosen equivalence of 2.

Definition 10 (Observational equivalence). Given a regime \(E\) of accessible experiments, two theories satisfy \(T_1 \sim_E T_2\) when they give identical predictions in \(E\). The relation \(\sim_E\) is an equivalence relation on theories, and the selection layer \(\mathcal{E}\) is the map whose induced quotient computes it.

The three layers of the assembly

The three layers of 5 are stipulated as separate maps, and the evidence base treats them as separate.

Under the architecture of 5, classification data determines neither realization nor selection. The architecture stipulates \(\mathcal{R}\) and \(\mathcal{E}\) as maps with separate domains and codomains, and the founding documents stipulate that several mathematically distinct theories may produce identical predictions within an experimentally accessible regime. The registry of the program, examined record by record, contains classification theorems, reconstruction theorems, and obstruction theorems, and it contains no observation functor and no construction of \(\mathcal{E}\): every record lives at the mathematical layer or at the interface between the mathematical and realization layers. Classification data therefore underdetermines empirical selection.

Empirical selection is not a corollary of classification. Let \(T_1\) and \(T_2\) be mathematically distinct theories whose predictions agree in an accessible regime \(E\), as the architecture stipulates; write \(T_1 \sim_E T_2\) for that agreement. A classification at \(\mathcal{M}\) distinguishes \(T_1\) from \(T_2\), so the classification map is not constant on \(\sim_E\)-classes and cannot compute them. Deciding \(\sim_E\) requires the map \(\mathcal{E}\), which the classification layer does not supply.

Remark 11. Row 21 records a proved parametric classification: quantum monotone metrics are classified by operator monotone functions [48]. The parameter class here is an infinite-dimensional class of functions, not the finite or discrete data of the other regime-1 rows, and uniqueness fails in the quantum case. The program periodic table lists the same domain among open classification problems because the classification is not a finite list. Both statements stand together: the classification theorem is proved, and the classification is not finite.

Within the evidence base, classification is sharp exactly where the invariant is finite or explicitly parameterized, and the classification content can be entirely global. Every regime-1 and regime-2 row of 3 has a finite or explicitly parameterized invariant, and the domains without such invariants are exactly the domains with no classification theorem. The global character of symplectic classification is Darboux’s theorem [6]: any two symplectic manifolds of the same dimension are locally symplectomorphic, so the local invariant is empty and the classifying data must be global. The contrast with Riemannian geometry, where curvature supplies local invariants, is structural rather than incidental.

Classification, parameterization, recognition

Three classification achievements are often conflated. A parameterization assigns uniform finite data to each object. A classification lists the objects exhaustively. A recognition procedure identifies an object algorithmically from companion data.

The classification of finite simple groups delivers the second achievement and only part of the third. The list of families and sporadic groups is complete [1], [2], and practical identification runs through companion data of atlas type: order, Schur multiplier, outer automorphism group, presentations, and character tables with power maps [3]. No clean uniform parameterization analogous to Dynkin diagrams is known [1]. The template program itself therefore sits in regime 2 of 7, and a periodic table of physical structures cannot expect more from its analogy than the template delivers.

The analogy between the two classification problems is architectural rather than evidentiary. The finite simple groups share one definition; physical systems have no universally accepted mathematical definition, and the schema of 1 is a candidate. The founding documents warn against assuming that physical theories possess a finite collection of exceptional objects analogous to the twenty-six sporadic groups [1]. Exceptional mathematics exists and the taxonomy must accommodate it: the exceptional Lie algebras [4], the type \(\mathrm{III}\) factors and the hyperfinite \(\mathrm{II}_1\) factor [8], the eight Thurston geometries [56], and the isocategorical group pairs [12]. Whether any two of the ten families of 1 are logically independent is open, and no theorem on record settles it.

Obstruction theorems

Obstructions are theorems. Seven of them are registered, and each forbids a classification objective that one might otherwise pursue.

Theorem 12 (Survey: non-classification outside tame regimes, assembled from McDuff, Adyan-Rabin, the wildness results, Tancer, Etingof-Gelaki, and Grochow-Qiao). The following classification objectives are forbidden by proved results. Separable type \(\mathrm{II}_1\) factors admit no total classification outside the amenable case, and their isomorphism relation admits no classification by countable invariants. Wild representation categories admit no finite-variety parameterization. Markov properties of finitely presented groups, including finiteness and simplicity, are algorithmically undecidable. Homeomorphism of compact four-manifolds is algorithmically unrecognizable in general. A finite group is not determined by its representation category as a plain tensor category. Group isomorphism admits no polynomial-time algorithm in wild regimes under standard complexity assumptions, while tame extensions do. Consequently any classification theorem in the program must either restrict its class to a tame regime or weaken its invariant notion, and a global periodic table covering all families cannot exist outside tame regimes.

Proof. The first statement is McDuff’s theorem: there are uncountably many non-isomorphic separable type \(\mathrm{II}_1\) factors [8], [60]. A total classification by a countable list of invariants would inject that uncountable family into a countable product of invariant values, which is impossible, and the Borel-reducibility analysis makes the failure precise for isomorphism of von Neumann algebras [55]. The second statement is the wildness line: every finite-dimensional algebra over an algebraically closed field is derived-tame or derived-wild with wildness preserved under degeneration [51], the rank-2 isomorphism classes of the generic determinant’s Cohen-Macaulay modules admit no finite-dimensional-variety parameterization [52], and no meaningful classification of compact \(C_p\)-equivariant \(H\mathbb{F}_p\)-modules exists at odd primes [54]. The third statement is the Adyan-Rabin theorem with the Collins extension [49]. The fourth statement is the unrecognizability of connected-sum homeomorphism for a specific four-manifold [50], set against the eight-geometry decomposition of closed three-manifolds [56], [63]. The fifth statement is the isocategorical-group construction: non-isomorphic finite groups carry equivalent representation categories as plain tensor categories [12]. The sixth statement is the tame/wild tractability boundary [53]. Each forbidden objective is a classification covering one of the named sectors, so each such classification would contradict the corresponding theorem. The conjunctive conclusion follows. ◻

What the obstructions permit

12 does not say that classification fails everywhere. It says that classification objectives must match regimes.

The amenable factors are completely classified by Murray-von Neumann type and Connes invariants [8], [58], [59], and the obstruction concerns only the non-amenable remainder. The classification theorem and the impossibility theorem sit side by side and refer to different classes. The anomaly sector is classified completely in regime 3: quantization exists exactly when the relevant cohomological obstruction classes vanish [19], so the classification content is the obstruction itself. An impossibility statement of this shape is a finished classification, not a gap. Dimension three admits a finite table of Thurston geometries [56], [63] while dimension four contains algorithmically unrecognizable instances [50], so finite tables exist only in tame regimes. The same contrast appears inside group theory: tame extensions admit polynomial-time isomorphism tests while wild regimes do not [53], and unknot recognition is decidable in principle with its complexity-class membership open [57], which places it in regime 7 and separates decidability from classification.

The register therefore has a constructive use. An obstruction names the restriction a classification theorem needs. McDuff’s theorem tells a would-be factor classification to restrict to the amenable class, and that restricted classification exists. Wildness tells a would-be module classification to restrict to the derived-tame side. The unrecognizability theorem tells a would-be four-manifold classification to restrict to decomposable classes. Each obstruction pairs with a restricted positive theorem, and that pairing is the working form of the program’s negative results.

Remark 13. The program founding documents warn against assuming that physical theories possess a finite collection of exceptional objects analogous to the twenty-six sporadic groups [1]. The sporadic groups exist and are exhaustive in the finite-simple setting, and the corresponding question for physical theories is open. The obstruction register shows what a periodic table must record where no table exists.

Relational characterization

Reconstruction from probes has a categorical form. The Yoneda embedding sends an object \(P\) to the functor \(\mathrm{Hom}(-, P)\), and the Yoneda lemma shows that an object of a locally small category is determined up to isomorphism by its relationships. The embedding is therefore a faithful mathematical representation of objects of \(\mathrm{Phys}\) through their relational profiles. Its physical interpretation is open.

The constraint on every relational argument in the program is stated verbatim: Yoneda does not, by itself, establish physical equivalence, empirical adequacy, or a classification theorem. The embedding supplies representability and nothing else. Physical equivalence requires the chosen relation of 2, empirical adequacy requires the selection layer, and a classification theorem requires invariants with completeness statements.

Two islands support the relational program. Concrete representability of categorical supermaps is proved whenever the theory admits suitable channel-state duality [37], [38]. The classical reconstruction pattern of Gelfand, Stone, and Naimark recovers objects from their probe algebras [39], [40]. Whether the two islands join into one principle is open, and no argument in this paper crosses that gap.

Theorem 14 (Survey: necessity of the structure hypotheses, assembled from Etingof-Gelaki, Doplicher-Roberts, Stinespring, and the supermap results). Every registered realization or identification theorem fails or becomes open as soon as its structure hypotheses are dropped. In particular: plain tensor equivalence of representation categories does not determine the finite group; Doplicher-Roberts reconstruction requires the symmetric structure and its companion hypotheses; Stinespring data is a normal form and its Kraus rank is not an isomorphism invariant of channels; supermap representability requires channel-state duality. Consequently any identification of systems in the program must declare the layers of structure it preserves, and an identification that forgets the monoidal or symmetric layer is refuted.

Proof. Isocategorical finite groups are non-isomorphic groups whose representation categories are equivalent as plain tensor categories [12], which refutes recovery of the group from plain tensor data and proves the last clause for that case. The Doplicher-Roberts statement is a theorem under its hypotheses, and no theorem on record covers the hypothesis-free version; the symmetric layer is load-bearing because its removal is exactly the case refuted by the counterexample. The Stinespring statement classifies completely positive maps up to minimal dilation, unique up to unitary, and Kraus rank is an invariant of that normal form only [10], [11], so any claim that treats it as an isomorphism invariant of channels asserts more than the theorem. Supermap representability is proved in settings with channel-state duality [37], [38], and the general case is open. Each clause is therefore either a counterexample or a recorded hypothesis gap, and the conjunctive conclusion follows. ◻

14 is the realization-layer form of the discipline that any equivalence record must state the structure it preserves. The braided case adds a further warning: fixed-point-net representation categories in braided Galois theory require extra soliton data beyond the braiding [21], so even a braided equivalence is not automatically sufficient.

The central test

The realization layer carries a central unanswered test. Can the proposed foundational structures reconstruct gauge connections and curvature, quantum states and observables, interacting Yang-Mills dynamics, and spacetime geometry together with gravitational dynamics, without assuming at the outset the spacetime geometry they aim to explain? Four constraints bound the current answer.

The finite-groupoid and weighted-nerve constructions of emergent spacetime are concrete, and their continuum convergence conditions are not established without complete proofs. The Ryu-Takayanagi formula is a proposal argued from the AdS/CFT correspondence and verified exactly only in the three-dimensional anti-de Sitter setting with two-dimensional conformal field theory [41]. Entanglement-wedge bulk reconstruction is a proved quantum-information theorem [42], so it lives at the interface of the mathematical and realization layers and is not a theorem about gravity. Tensor-network constructions prove entropy relations inside exactly solvable toys and not inside the physical duality [44], and the statement that entanglement builds spacetime is an argument rather than a theorem [45].

The honest summary of the realization layer is therefore asymmetric. Reconstruction from probes is strong where the probes are algebraic or categorical. Reconstruction of interacting dynamics and of spacetime itself from primitive data remains open.

The empirical layer

The empirical layer \(\mathcal{E}\) is where mathematical classification stops and observational selection begins.

Theorem 15 (Survey: classical uniqueness and quantum parametricity, assembled from Chentsov and Petz-Sudar). The Fisher metric is the unique Riemannian metric, up to constant factor, that is monotone under stochastic maps. Quantum monotone metrics are classified by operator monotone functions, a parametric family containing more than one element. Classical uniqueness therefore does not extend to the quantum case, and any quantum distinguishability criterion must either select within the parametric family and justify the selection or carry the family as data.

Proof. The first statement is Chentsov’s theorem [48]. The second is the Petz-Sudar classification [48]. The parameterizing class of operator monotone functions contains more than one element; for instance the identity function and the affine function \(f(x) = (x+1)/2\) are both operator monotone and give different metrics. The uniqueness statement is therefore false in the quantum case, and the last clause follows: a criterion that names a single quantum monotone metric makes an additional choice beyond the classification theorem. ◻

15 fixes what empirical distinguishability can borrow from information geometry. In the classical case the statistical distance is canonical. In the quantum case the statistical distance is a choice inside a proved parameterization, so an observational equivalence relation built on such distances must state the choice.

Observational equivalence

Two theories that differ as mathematical descriptions may be empirically indistinguishable in an accessible regime, so observational equivalence is a further quotient that the mathematical classification does not compute. Conversely an equivalence that forgets the monoidal or symmetric layer is too coarse to be a correct identification at all, because isocategorical groups refute it [12]. Any observational criterion must therefore be fine enough to respect the preserved-structure requirement and coarse enough to quotient the empirically indistinguishable.

Formalizing that criterion needs an observation functor with four pieces of data: a domain of theories, a codomain of observational constraints or predictions, a composition rule with coarse-graining, and the induced equivalence classes of 10. The record contains none of the four. Whether observational equivalence composes in interacting regimes is unstudied, and the positive question of what makes two descriptions one physical system is open: the isocategorical counterexample shows what is insufficient, and no reconstruction criterion for sameness exists.

The announced existence work explicitly omits the mass gap, lacks public artifacts, and awaits independent verification [24], [25], [26]; it carries the status existence claimed; manuscript and formalization withheld; verification pending. The remaining entries restrict their scope to lattice approximations without establishing the continuum limit [30], [31], [32], confine their arguments to three dimensions [33], or remain unverified or retracted [28], [29]. The Clay problem page lists the problem as unsolved [23]. Because constructive field theory currently lacks a demonstrated continuum mass gap, the realization layer for four-dimensional non-Abelian gauge theory remains open.

A pre-claim baseline makes the ladder vivid. The 2026 survey of rigorous approaches to quantum field theory records that rigorous renormalization has never been made rigorous in the needed generality and that on the mass gap no clear path is known [27]. The October 2026 existence announcement postdates that survey, contradicts its existence-side assessment, and confirms its mass-gap-side assessment [24]. Independent verification before a standing committee is the step that would move the claim up the ladder [25].

The register also contains frontier items with lighter status. Higher-form DHR theory exhibits an extra \(Z_2\) sector not realizable by Lagrangian gauge models, recorded from a single source [20]. A braided Doplicher-Roberts program constructs a global quantum gauge group [22]. Braided Galois theory requires soliton data beyond the braiding [21]. Each is research input, and none is load-bearing in this assembly.

The structural classification conjecture

The organizing statement of the program is the following.

Conjecture 16 (Structural classification of admissible physical systems). Let \(\mathrm{Phys}_{\mathrm{adm}}\) denote the class of mathematically admissible physical systems satisfying explicitly stated consistency conditions. There exists a collection of fundamental structural families \(\mathfrak{F} = \{\mathfrak{F}_\alpha\}_{\alpha \in A}\) and a set of admissible constructions \(\mathfrak{C}\) such that \[\mathrm{Phys}_{\mathrm{adm}} \simeq \langle \mathfrak{F}_\alpha \rangle_{\mathfrak{C}}.\]

The conjecture carries five proof obligations; the scoreboard in 6 is the state of the program.

Table 6. The five proof obligations of 16 and their state. Four are open and one is partial.
Obligation Content State
Completeness Every admissible system is represented in the family closure Open
Soundness Every generated structure satisfies the admissibility conditions Open
Equivalence Different representations of one system are identified correctly Open
Structural characterization The fundamental families have precise definitions Partial
Decomposition Systems in the targeted classes are built from the families Open in general

Definition 17 (Constituent invariant record). Let \(P\) admit a decomposition \(P \simeq \mathcal{F}(I_1, \ldots, I_n)\) with irreducible constituents. The constituent invariant record is \(\mu(P) = (\mathcal{F}, \{\mathcal{I}(I_i)\}, g_P)\): the construction data, the multiset of invariant records of 9 of the constituents, and the gluing datum \(g_P\) that \(\mathcal{F}\) requires to assemble them. The gluing datum is extension data of the construction: a group extension \(1 \to N \to G \to Q \to 1\) is recorded by a class in \(H^2(Q, Z(N))\) in addition to the invariant records of \(N\) and \(Q\), and other constructions carry the analogous clutching or k-invariant data. Where the decomposition is unique up to order and up to equivalence of constituents, \(\mu\) is well defined on \(\simeq\)-classes.

Proposition 18 (Completeness of the constituent record for two constructions). Let \(P\) be an object assembled by one of two operations. In case (a) \(P\) is a finite-dimensional complex \(C^*\)-algebra written as a direct sum \(P \cong \bigoplus_{i=1}^{k} M_{n_i}(\mathbb{C})\), with constituents classified by their block sizes. In case (b) \(P\) is a group extension \(1 \to N \to P \to Q \to 1\) with a fixed action of \(Q\) on \(N\), with \(N\) and \(Q\) classified up to isomorphism and the extension class \(g_P\) recorded in \(H^2(Q, Z(N))\). In case (a) the multiset of block sizes is a complete invariant of \(P\) up to \(*\)-isomorphism. In case (b) the pair consisting of the invariant records of \(N\) and \(Q\) and the extension class \(g_P\) is a complete invariant of \(P\) up to isomorphism of extensions. In both cases the constituent record of 17 is complete, and the construction maps neither into theories nor into observation data.

Proof. In case (a) the block sizes are the dimensions of the simple modules of \(P\), which are invariant under \(*\)-isomorphism, and they determine the algebra by the structure theorem [9], [39]; this is 21. In case (b) the classification of group extensions with a fixed action states that isomorphism classes of extensions of \(Q\) by \(N\) correspond bijectively to classes in \(H^2(Q, Z(N))\), and an isomorphism of extensions preserves the class while a shared class builds an isomorphism of extensions [65]. In both cases the record \(\mu(P)\) therefore determines \(P\) up to the stated equivalence. Neither construction outputs a theory or an observation, so the non-collapse argument of Section 3 leaves realization and selection unconstructed. ◻

Remark 19 (Generation and classification). The five obligations concern generation, admissibility, identification, family definitions, and decomposition, and 12 concerns classification by finite or parametric invariants and by algorithms. The two objectives do not contradict each other: finitely presented groups are generated from cyclic groups by free products and quotients, and their Markov properties are algorithmically undecidable [49]. The objectives meet only in regimes 1 and 2 of 3, where constituents carry classifying invariants and 18 applies. In the sectors of 12 the classification-layer objective of 5 is unattainable without restricting the class or weakening the invariant notion.

18 covers the two constructions where extension theory is established. The analogous completeness statement for the remaining admissible constructions is conditional on the five obligations of 16, of which one is partial and four are open, and on uniqueness of decomposition, which the program leaves open; it is not claimed here. Its conclusion would stop at the mathematical layer. The regime analysis of 4 bounds its scope further: inside a target class, the classifying data must live in a regime of 3 where invariants are finite or explicitly parameterized, and outside those regimes 12 forbids the classification the conjecture would deliver.

Remark 20. The conjecture may also fail. The founding program records that proving either a complete classification of a specified class or the impossibility of natural classification objectives without additional restrictions is a meaningful mathematical result. The obstruction register already delivers the second kind of result in six sectors.

Worked examples

The examples trace one classical, one quantum, and one gauge system through the three layers. Each separates \(\mathcal{M}\), \(\mathcal{R}\), and \(\mathcal{E}\) and shows where the proved content ends.

Finite-dimensional quantum systems

Let \(A = \mathbb{C} \oplus M_2(\mathbb{C})\), the complex \(C^*\)-algebra with one one-dimensional block and one two-dimensional block. At the mathematical layer, \(A\) is classified by its dimension vector, the multiset of matrix sizes, here \(\{1, 2\}\): any finite-dimensional complex \(C^*\)-algebra is isomorphic to a direct sum of matrix blocks, and the multiset of block sizes determines the algebra up to \(*\)-isomorphism [9], [39].

Lemma 21 (Uniqueness of the block decomposition). Every finite-dimensional complex \(C^*\)-algebra \(A\) admits a decomposition \(A \cong \bigoplus_{i=1}^{k} M_{n_i}(\mathbb{C})\) with the multiset \(\{n_1, \ldots, n_k\}\) uniquely determined by \(A\). For the example algebra the multiset is \(\{1, 2\}\), and any decomposition into irreducible blocks realizes it.

Proof. Existence is the structure theorem for finite-dimensional \(C^*\)-algebras [9]. Uniqueness of the multiset follows because the block sizes are recovered from \(A\) as the dimensions of its simple modules, and simple modules are invariant under \(*\)-isomorphism. For \(A = \mathbb{C} \oplus M_2(\mathbb{C})\) the simple modules have dimensions one and two, so every decomposition into irreducible summands has block sizes \(\{1, 2\}\) up to order. ◻

The blocks \(M_{n_i}(\mathbb{C})\) are the irreducible constituents under the direct-sum construction, so this example displays decomposition and irreducibility exactly as 4 requires, and 21 shows the decomposition unique in this restricted class.

At the realization layer, \(A\) acts on a Hilbert space and states are density matrices on \(\mathbb{C} \oplus \mathbb{C}^2\). The representation is the standard one from the Gelfand-Naimark embedding [39], which is a representation theorem and not a classification theorem; the classification content is the dimension vector from the layer above.

At the empirical layer, measurement statistics on states define distinguishability. The classical statistical distance is canonical by 15, and the quantum statistical distance is one choice inside the parametric family of operator monotone functions. Two admissible choices give two inequivalent distinguishability structures on the same classified algebra, so the empirical layer carries data the mathematical layer does not determine.

Classical mechanics

Let \((M, \omega)\) be a symplectic manifold with Hamiltonian dynamics. At the mathematical layer, Darboux’s theorem removes all local invariants [6], so the classifying data is global: the topology of \(\omega\) and its cohomology class are candidate data, and Poisson geometry adds Weinstein splitting, the symplectic-leaf foliation, and coadjoint orbits for the Kirillov-Kostant-Souriau case [7], [62]. Local classification is complete and trivial, and the classification content is entirely global.

At the realization layer, Hamiltonian vector fields realize the dynamics and the observable algebra is the algebra of smooth functions on \(M\). At the empirical layer, the Fisher metric is the unique monotone metric up to constant factor [48], so classical statistical distinguishability is canonical. The bridge from this family to the quantum family through deformation quantization is a programmatic link and not an equivalence theorem, so no classical-to-quantum conclusion crosses the layer boundary.

Gauge theory and the claim register

Let \(P \to X\) be a principal \(G\)-bundle with connection. At the mathematical layer, principal bundles are classified by homotopy classes of maps into the classifying space \(BG\), and characteristic classes are pullbacks of universal classes [17], [18]. Anomalies are classified as obstruction classes: quantization exists exactly when the relevant classes vanish [19]. This is regime 3, complete as obstruction, and it is the volume’s sharpest example of classification content that is an impossibility statement.

At the realization layer, the reconstruction of an interacting four-dimensional quantum field theory from such data is claimed rather than established. The announced existence result carries the status existence claimed; manuscript and formalization withheld; verification pending [24], [25]. At the empirical layer, the mass gap remains open [23], lattice theorems hold only in restricted regimes [30], [31], [32], and no entry of 5 closes the continuum limit. The example therefore shows the three layers in three different states: proved at \(\mathcal{M}\), claimed at \(\mathcal{R}\), open at \(\mathcal{E}\).

Requirements for a unified framework

A unified structural theory of mathematical physics would need eight deliverables, and the assembly names each one precisely.

Table 7. The eight deliverables a unified framework requires, with the current state of each.
Deliverable Current state
Admissibility conditions defining the target class Unfixed, so statements over the class are conditional
Preservation laws and composition rules for morphisms Unfixed, so the ambient category is a schema
A chosen physical equivalence with declared preserved structure Open, constrained by the isocategorical counterexample
A constructed realization map from structures to theories Stipulated only
A constructed selection map from theories to observations Not constructed in the record
Regime-appropriate objectives Established by the regime analysis of 3 and by 12
A verification layer for the registered proofs No record carries a completed proof-assistant check
Criteria for physical validation of the architecture Open, level named but its criteria unstated

The first three deliverables are definitional. Until the admissibility conditions are fixed, every theorem over the admissible class is conditional on the chosen conditions. Until the morphism rules are fixed, the ambient category is a schema, and any theorem must state which morphism classes are in force. The equivalence must name the structure it preserves, and the isocategorical counterexample shows what happens otherwise [12].

The fourth and fifth deliverables are constructions, and neither exists in the record. The realization map is stipulated as an arrow in the architecture and never built; the selection map has no definition of its observation object, no domain, no codomain, and no composition rule with coarse-graining. Until both are built, the identity of 18 stops at the mathematical layer.

The sixth deliverable is what this paper supplies. Restricted classification theorems are the right objective where invariants are finite or explicitly parameterized, and obstruction theorems are the right objective everywhere else. A rigorous impossibility result is a valuable outcome of the program, not a consolation.

The seventh deliverable is verification. No proof in the series has been checked by a proof-assistant kernel. The executable taxonomy code in the source directory is computational evidence: it is validated against stated specifications and has not been checked by a kernel. The verification directory records ten mechanization targets matching the definitions and theorems above. Mechanization is future work.

The eighth deliverable is the hardest. Physical validation would require empirical criteria that the architecture could pass and its rivals fail. The corpus names the level and does not fix its criteria. Until those criteria exist, the architecture is a mathematical research program with proved components and an unvalidated assembly.

Limitations

Four limitations bound this synthesis. The assembly inherits the statuses of the program registry, and any status revision propagates. Three entries rest on single sources and were not independently re-verified here: the extra \(Z_2\) sector of higher-form DHR theory [20], the journal-published mass-gap claim [28], and the Borel-reducibility obstruction announced at the survey level [55]. The literature survey behind the registry ran on the arXiv API plus known-URL fetches, so non-arXiv venues may be under-covered, and absence of a citation here is not evidence of absence. The regime taxonomy organizes the registered domains and proves a coverage statement about them; it does not classify all of mathematics.

The formal statements of this paper are assembly results: coverage statements about a finite registry, reductions to the registered impossibility theorems, necessity statements about structure hypotheses, and one conditional reduction. No new classifying invariant is introduced.

Conclusion

The assembly establishes four things. Every domain of the program comparison spine sits in exactly one classification regime, fifteen of them with finite or explicitly parameterized invariants and complete classification theorems. Seven obstruction theorems forbid classification in the wild, non-amenable, and undecidable sectors, so classification objectives must match regimes. Every realization and identification theorem fails or becomes open when its structure hypotheses are dropped, so identifications must declare preserved structure. And the structural classification conjecture, under its five obligations, would complete the mathematical layer for a targeted class while leaving realization and selection untouched.

What remains uncertain is equally sharp. The admissibility conditions are unfixed. The physical equivalence is unchosen. The realization and selection maps are stipulated and not constructed. The announced four-dimensional existence result carries the status existence claimed; manuscript and formalization withheld; verification pending, and the mass gap is open. No record carries a completed computer-checked proof. The central test of the realization layer, reconstruction of interacting dynamics and spacetime without presupposing spacetime, is unanswered.

What follows is the founding program’s own calibration. The architecture is precise enough to organize a substantial long-term mathematical research program, and its components produce valuable results even if a universal classification theorem proves unattainable. The program should be prepared to prove either that a specified class admits a complete classification or that certain natural classification objectives are impossible without additional restrictions. Both are meaningful mathematical results, and the obstruction register has already begun to deliver the second.
