Abstract

Structural classification of physical systems requires a precise account of irreducibility: the objects counted as basic, the constructions that assemble systems from them, and the uniqueness, or absence of uniqueness, of the resulting decomposition. This paper develops that account within a categorical schema of physical systems whose composition data determine what irreducibility means. The central positive result is the complete decomposition theory of unital finite-dimensional complex C*-algebras: every such algebra is a finite direct sum of matrix algebras, the multiset of matrix sizes classifies it up to *-isomorphism, and unital *-homomorphisms between decomposed algebras are classified exactly by multiplicity matrices with explicit arithmetic constraints. The paper then maps the limits of this pattern. Kraus rank is an invariant of the Stinespring normal form and not an isomorphism invariant of channels. Interacting systems admit no unique decomposition outside restricted subclasses. Wild representation categories forbid finite-dimensional parameterization of their indecomposables. Reconstruction theorems recover objects from probes under stated hypotheses, and those hypotheses carry the content. Computational evidence in an executable taxonomy layer and in enumeration experiments is recorded as evidence and never as proof. The results support decomposition theorems only in restricted, tame regimes.

Introduction

Classification programs in mathematics succeed where three conditions hold at once: the objects are well defined, the equivalence is fixed, and a finite or parametric invariant separates the objects up to that equivalence. The classification of finite simple groups is the model case, and it is also a warning, because the classification is complete while a clean uniform parameterization of the simple groups remains out of reach [15]. A structural classification of mathematical representations of physical systems would need all three conditions, and the third is where decomposition enters: a classification by constituents presumes that systems decompose into irreducibles, that the decomposition is unique enough to define invariants, and that the constructions assembling systems from irreducibles are understood.

A physical system is recorded as a tuple \(P = (X, \mathcal{O}, \mathcal{D}, \mathcal{S}, \mathcal{C})\) of state space, observables, dynamics, symmetry data, and composition rules, and the classification problem is \(\mathrm{Classify}(\mathrm{Ob}(\mathrm{Phys})/{\simeq})\) for a chosen equivalence \(\simeq\). The present paper develops the machinery that turns candidate structural families into a classification: irreducibility, decomposition, admissible constructions, and closure under those constructions.

The program’s long-term target is the identity \(\mathrm{Phys}_{\mathrm{adm}} \simeq \langle \mathfrak{F}_1, \dots, \mathfrak{F}_n \rangle_{\mathcal{C}}\), the statement that every admissible system is assembled from the fundamental families by the admissible constructions. That identity is a research objective and not an assumed result. The results below are narrower and concrete: existence and uniqueness of decomposition in one tame class, and theorem-marked boundaries beyond it.

Irreducibility depends strictly on the composition operation assigned to a system, and the relativity is visible in concrete examples. Under direct sum, unital finite-dimensional complex C*-algebras decompose uniquely into simple matrix blocks classified completely by their dimension vectors, and their unital *-homomorphisms correspond bijectively to integer multiplicity matrices with explicit arithmetic constraints. This decomposition theory is an isolated tame regime. The Stinespring and Kraus descriptions of completely positive maps yield normal forms rather than isomorphism classifications. Interacting systems admit no unique decomposition outside restricted subclasses. Wild representation categories forbid finite-dimensional parameterization of their indecomposables. The reconstruction pattern, in which an object is recovered from its probes, is developed and separated from classification: commutative C*-algebras are recovered from their spectra, and symmetry data from superselection categories, both under hypotheses that cannot be dropped.

The technical development concerns the observable-algebra layer of the tuple: the classification theorems below classify algebras and their morphisms, and the state-space layer enters only where decomposition acts on states. Dynamics, symmetry actions, and coupling data are named in the definitions and left untreated here. Every statement about interacting systems is restricted to subclasses, and every result that leans on an unproved premise is marked conditional. A rigorous impossibility theorem is as valuable here as a classification theorem, and both kinds of content appear below.

Mathematical framework

The definitions below fix the notation used throughout. They are stipulative where no theorem is cited: they are vocabulary adopted for this analysis rather than consequences of deeper axioms.

Definition 1 (Physical system, composition data). A physical system is a tuple \(P = (X, \mathcal{O}, \mathcal{D}, \mathcal{S}, \mathcal{C})\) consisting of a state space \(X\), an observable algebra or collection \(\mathcal{O}\), dynamical laws \(\mathcal{D}\), symmetry and constraint data \(\mathcal{S}\), and composition and interaction rules \(\mathcal{C}\). The component \(\mathcal{C}\) specifies how \(P\) composes with other systems: tensor products, coupling terms, interfacing rules.

The five components need not be independent, and the tuple is a candidate schema rather than a settled definition of every possible physical system. The choice of \(\mathcal{C}\) determines everything that follows.

Definition 2 (Irreducibility relative to \(\mathcal{C}\)). Fix a composition operation \(\ast\) named by \(\mathcal{C}\), with its unit object: the zero system for direct sum, the one-dimensional system for tensor product. A system \(P\) is irreducible relative to \(\ast\) when \(P\) admits no decomposition \(P \simeq \ast(P_1, P_2)\) with both \(P_1\) and \(P_2\) nonisomorphic to the unit. The class of irreducibles for \(\ast\) is \[\mathfrak{J}_{\ast} = \{ P : P \text{ is irreducible relative to } \ast \}.\]

Irreducibility is therefore never an absolute property of a tuple. It is a property of the pair \((P, \ast)\), and changing the composition operation changes the class of basic objects. Section 3 exhibits a concrete pair of systems on which two natural composition operations return opposite answers.

Definition 3 (Decomposition). A decomposition of \(P\) is an expression \(P \simeq \mathcal{F}(I_1, \dots, I_n)\) with each \(I_i\) irreducible relative to the composition operation in force and \(\mathcal{F}\) an admissible construction. A decomposition is unique in a class when any two decompositions of a system in that class differ by a permutation of isomorphic factors and by the ambient equivalence \(\simeq\).

Definition 4 (Admissible constructions). The admissible constructions \(\mathcal{F}\) are the permitted operations assembling systems from simpler ones: tensor product, direct sum, extension (group, algebra, field-theoretic), limits and colimits including the limiting procedures of the morphism layer, factorization of a representation into factors, and quotient or symmetry reduction. Each use of a construction must state the composition data it contributes to \(\mathcal{C}\).

The list is stipulative, and every uniqueness claim in this paper is uniqueness relative to a named construction. The direct sum and the tensor product behave differently in every respect that follows.

Definition 5 (Closure). Given a collection \(\mathfrak{F}\) of families and a set of constructions \(\mathcal{C}\), the closure \(\langle \mathfrak{F} \rangle_{\mathcal{C}}\) is the smallest class of systems containing \(\mathfrak{F}\) and closed under the constructions \(\mathcal{C}\).

The long-term structural generation statement is the identity \(\mathrm{Phys}_{\mathrm{adm}} \simeq \langle \mathfrak{F} \rangle_{\mathcal{C}}\). Its status is conjectural. The proved domains in which generation statements hold are the restricted ones treated below.

Definition 6 (Dimension vector). For a unital finite-dimensional complex C*-algebra presented as a direct sum \(A \cong \bigoplus_{i=1}^{k} M_{n_i}(\mathbb{C})\), the dimension vector of \(A\) is the multiset \(\mathbf{n}(A) = \{n_1, \dots, n_k\}\) of matrix sizes, equivalently the unordered list of the dimensions of the simple \(A\)-modules.

Remark 7 (Two composition operations on operator-algebra systems). For systems whose observable algebra is a unital finite-dimensional complex C*-algebra, two composition operations are natural: the direct sum \(A \oplus B\) of observable algebras, which models superselection, and the tensor product \(A \otimes B\), which models subsystems. The irreducibles of the first operation are the simple blocks \(M_n(\mathbb{C})\). The irreducibles of the second are different. The distinction is not cosmetic; it decides what counts as a basic object.

Remark 8 (Equivalence discipline). Any equivalence \(\simeq\) used to identify systems or factors in this paper is a *-isomorphism of observable algebras, unless another relation is named. In concrete terms for the classes at hand: two decomposed algebras are identified exactly when a bijection of their block lists matches matrix sizes blockwise and implements a *-isomorphism. Equivalences that forget algebraic structure are not used to establish classification statements, and no identification of mathematical isomorphism with physical or empirical equivalence is attempted here.

Classification, parameterization, recognition

Three achievements are separated throughout this paper because the classification of finite simple groups separates them in practice. A classification is an exhaustive list of isomorphism types. A parameterization is a uniform finite or algebraic description of those types, in the style of Dynkin diagrams for semisimple Lie algebras. A recognition procedure is an algorithm that identifies a presented object by computing its invariants. The finite simple groups are classified, are not uniformly parameterized in the Dynkin sense [15], and are recognized in practice by companion data of the kind recorded in group atlases: order, character table, outer automorphism group, presentations. The decomposition theorem of the next section delivers all three achievements at once for its class.

Decomposition existence

The central positive result of this paper is the block decomposition of unital finite-dimensional complex C*-algebras. This section proves it in full, because the classification package built on it needs both halves: existence of the decomposition and uniqueness of the invariants.

Semisimplicity and blocks

The block decomposition combines two facts from different centuries. Artin-Wedderburn reduces a semisimple Artinian algebra to matrix algebras over division algebras; the C*-structure then forces the division algebras to be \(\mathbb{C}\) and the central idempotents to be self-adjoint. The first fact is algebra, the second is analysis, and the block decomposition sits exactly at their join [14].

Lemma 9 (No nilpotent radical). Let \(A\) be a finite-dimensional C*-algebra over \(\mathbb{C}\). The Jacobson radical \(J(A)\) is zero.

Proof. \(A\) is finite-dimensional, hence Artinian, and \(J(A)\) is a nilpotent ideal. The radical of a C*-algebra is a *-ideal, so it is closed under adjoints, and every \(x \in J(A)\) has a Cartesian decomposition \(x = a + ib\) with \(a = (x + x^*)/2\) and \(b = (x - x^*)/(2i)\), both self-adjoint elements of \(J(A)\), since \(a^* = a\), \(b^* = b\), and \(a + ib = x\). The radical of an Artinian ring consists of nilpotent elements, and a sum or scalar multiple of nilpotent elements in a nilpotent ideal is nilpotent, so \(a\) and \(b\) are nilpotent. A self-adjoint nilpotent element of a C*-algebra is zero: by the spectral theorem the spectrum of \(a\) is \(\{0\}\), and a self-adjoint element equals its spectral integral, so \(a = 0\). Thus \(a = b = 0\) and \(x = 0\). ◻

Lemma 10 (Simple blocks are matrix algebras). Let \(B\) be a finite-dimensional simple C*-algebra over \(\mathbb{C}\). Then \(B \cong M_n(\mathbb{C})\) for some \(n \geq 1\).

Proof. By the Artin-Wedderburn theorem [14] \(B \cong M_n(D)\) for a finite-dimensional division algebra \(D\) over \(\mathbb{C}\). Every element \(d \in D\) generates a commutative subalgebra \(\mathbb{C}[d]\), which is a finite field extension of \(\mathbb{C}\) and therefore equals \(\mathbb{C}\), because \(\mathbb{C}\) is algebraically closed. Hence \(D = \mathbb{C} \cdot 1\) and \(B \cong M_n(\mathbb{C})\). ◻

Theorem 11 (Block decomposition). Let \(A\) be a unital finite-dimensional complex C*-algebra of dimension \(d\). Then there exist \(k \geq 1\) and integers \(n_1, \dots, n_k \geq 1\) with \(\sum_{i=1}^{k} n_i^2 = d\) and a *-isomorphism \[A \cong \bigoplus_{i=1}^{k} M_{n_i}(\mathbb{C}).\] The multiset \(\mathbf{n}(A) = \{n_1, \dots, n_k\}\) is unique up to permutation, and \(k = \dim_{\mathbb{C}} Z(A)\).

Proof. By 9 the algebra \(A\) is semisimple, so by Artin-Wedderburn \(A \cong \bigoplus_{i=1}^{k} M_{n_i}(D_i)\) with \(D_i\) finite-dimensional division algebras over \(\mathbb{C}\). The decomposition is given by the primitive central idempotents \(e_1, \dots, e_k\) of \(A\), which are uniquely determined by \(A\). Each \(e_i\) is self-adjoint: it is central, so \(e_i^*\) is also a central idempotent and \(e_i\) is normal; a normal idempotent of a C*-algebra has spectrum contained in \(\{0, 1\}\) and equals its spectral integral \(e_i = 0 \cdot P_0 + 1 \cdot P_1 = P_1\), a projection. Then \(A = \bigoplus_i e_i A e_i\) is a direct sum of *-ideals, each simple as an algebra, and 10 identifies \(e_i A e_i \cong M_{n_i}(\mathbb{C})\) as algebras. It remains to match the involutions: every C*-involution on \(M_n(\mathbb{C})\) is unitarily conjugate to the standard conjugate transpose, so composing each block isomorphism with a blockwise unitary adjustment yields a *-isomorphism \(A \cong \bigoplus_i M_{n_i}(\mathbb{C})\) with the adjoint taken blockwise. Counting dimensions gives \(\sum_i n_i^2 = \dim_{\mathbb{C}} A = d\), and the center is \(\bigoplus_i \mathbb{C} e_i\), so \(k = \dim_{\mathbb{C}} Z(A)\).

For uniqueness, the simple left \(A\)-modules are exactly the modules \(\mathbb{C}^{n_i}\) obtained by composing a projection \(A \to M_{n_i}(\mathbb{C})\) with the standard module of \(M_{n_i}(\mathbb{C})\). Distinct blocks give non-isomorphic simple modules because a simple module kills all but one primitive central idempotent. Hence the multiset of simple module dimensions is \(\{n_1, \dots, n_k\}\), and it is an isomorphism invariant. Any *-isomorphism \(A \cong A'\) carries simple modules to simple modules, so \(\mathbf{n}(A) = \mathbf{n}(A')\) as multisets. ◻

Corollary 12 (Classification of the class). Two unital finite-dimensional complex C*-algebras are *-isomorphic if and only if their dimension vectors agree as multisets. The dimension vector is a complete invariant of the class.

The corollary is the strongest classification statement this paper offers for a whole class of systems. It classifies unital finite-dimensional complex C*-algebras up to *-isomorphism of observable algebras. It classifies neither states nor dynamics nor symmetry actions nor composition, and the distinction is essential: an observable algebra is one component of the tuple \(P = (X, \mathcal{O}, \mathcal{D}, \mathcal{S}, \mathcal{C})\), and which additional structures are needed to classify physical systems rather than observable algebras remains open.

Remark 13 (Unique decomposition in the categorical sense). 11 places the category of unital finite-dimensional complex C*-algebras among the additive categories with the Krull-Schmidt property: every object is a finite direct sum of indecomposable objects, and the decomposition is unique up to permutation and isomorphism of summands. The property is special. Tensor decomposition of objects in the same category does not inherit it: \(M_4(\mathbb{C})\) factors as \(M_2 \otimes M_2\) and as \(M_4 \otimes \mathbb{C}\), and uniqueness statements about tensor factors live in the monoidal structure, not in the additive one.

Relativity of irreducibility

The block decomposition settles irreducibility for the direct sum. The tensor product asks a different question, and the answers differ.

Example 14 (One system, two answers). Consider a two-qubit system with observable algebra \(M_4(\mathbb{C})\). Under the direct sum composition, \(M_4(\mathbb{C})\) is irreducible: it is simple, it has no nontrivial central idempotent, and its dimension vector is \(\{4\}\). Under the tensor product composition, the same algebra factors as \(M_2(\mathbb{C}) \otimes M_2(\mathbb{C}) \cong M_4(\mathbb{C})\) with two irreducible tensor factors. The dimension vector of the tensor product is the pairwise product multiset of the factor vectors: the product multiset of \(\{2\}\) and \(\{2\}\) is \(\{4\}\), and the tensor factorization is invisible to the direct-sum invariant. Irreducibility is a property of the pair \((P, \ast)\).

Example 15 (Superselection and subsystems). The algebra \(M_2(\mathbb{C}) \oplus M_2(\mathbb{C})\) decomposes as a direct sum into two simple blocks, the standard model of two superselection sectors, each carrying a qubit observable algebra. The same algebra is not a tensor product of two matrix algebras of equal size: a tensor decomposition \(M_a \otimes M_b \cong M_{ab}\) is simple and has dimension vector \(\{ab\}\), while \(M_2 \oplus M_2\) has dimension vector \(\{2,2\}\) and total dimension \(8 = 2^2 + 2^2 \neq 2^3\). Direct-sum decomposition detects superselection, tensor decomposition detects subsystems, and neither detects the other.

States across blocks

The decomposition of an observable algebra induces a decomposition of its state space, and the induced decomposition is unique in the same restricted sense.

Definition 16 (State). A state on a unital C*-algebra \(A\) is a linear functional \(\omega \colon A \to \mathbb{C}\) that is positive and normalized by \(\omega(1) = 1\). Write \(S(A)\) for the set of states of \(A\).

Proposition 17 (States on a direct sum). Let \(A = A_1 \oplus A_2\) with unital summands \(A_1, A_2\) and central block projections \(p_1, p_2\). Every state \(\omega\) of \(A\) has a unique expression \[\omega = \lambda \, \omega_1 + (1 - \lambda) \, \omega_2\] where \(\lambda = \omega(p_1) \in [0,1]\), where \(\omega_i\) is the restriction of \(\omega\) to \(A_i\) normalized to a state of \(A_i\) when \(\omega(p_i) > 0\), and the term is omitted when its weight is zero. Consequently \(S(A)\) is the convex join of \(S(A_1)\) and \(S(A_2)\).

Proof. Write \(a = p_1 a p_1 + p_2 a p_2\) for \(a \in A\). Positivity of \(\omega\) gives \(0 \leq \omega(p_i) \leq 1\) and \(\omega(p_1) + \omega(p_2) = 1\). Set \(\lambda = \omega(p_1)\); when \(\lambda \in (0,1)\) define \(\omega_i(a) = \omega(p_i a p_i)/\lambda_i\) with \(\lambda_1 = \lambda\) and \(\lambda_2 = 1 - \lambda\). Each \(\omega_i\) is positive and normalized, and \(\omega\) is recovered as the convex combination above. Uniqueness of the weights follows from \(\omega(p_1) = \lambda\) and uniqueness of the normalized restrictions follows from the formula. The convex-join description is the standard identification of a convex set built by joining two convex sets along their extreme points. ◻

Example 18 (State space of two superselection sectors). For \(A = M_2(\mathbb{C}) \oplus M_2(\mathbb{C})\), each block state space is a Bloch ball and \(S(A)\) is the join of two Bloch balls: a state is a choice of sector weight \(\lambda \in [0,1]\) and a Bloch vector in each sector. The direct-sum decomposition of the algebra is exactly the superselection decomposition of the state space, and no information is lost or doubled in passing between the two descriptions.

Existence beyond the finite-dimensional case

Two further existence results are recorded because the later sections compare against them. They are background theorems of operator algebra, used here as reference points and not as dependencies of the classification statements above.

The first is the direct-integral decomposition of von Neumann algebras: every von Neumann algebra is a direct integral of factors over a measure space, and the decomposition is essentially unique up to measure-class isomorphism of the base [13]. Existence of a decomposition into indecomposable pieces is therefore available in that setting. The second is the negative theorem that closes the classification story for the indecomposable pieces: there are uncountably many pairwise non-isomorphic separable type \(\mathrm{II}_1\) factors [11]. A global classification of factors is therefore impossible in the stated generality, and any decomposition program that hopes to finish with a parameterized list of indecomposables must restrict its class. Existence of a decomposition and classification of its pieces are different achievements, and the second one fails here.

Morphisms and multiplicity data

A classification of objects is worth little if the morphisms between them are opaque. For decomposed finite-dimensional C*-algebras the morphisms admit an exact combinatorial description, and the description is a second existence-and-uniqueness theorem in a restricted setting.

Definition 19 (Multiplicity matrix). Let \(A \cong \bigoplus_{i=1}^{k} M_{n_i}(\mathbb{C})\) and \(B \cong \bigoplus_{j=1}^{\ell} M_{m_j}(\mathbb{C})\). A multiplicity matrix from \(A\) to \(B\) is a matrix \(R = (r^{(i)}_j)\) of nonnegative integers with \[k^{(i)}_j = n_i \, r^{(i)}_j \qquad \text{and} \qquad \sum_{i=1}^{k} k^{(i)}_j = m_j \quad \text{for each } j.\] Write \(k(R) = (k^{(i)}_j)\) for the induced rank matrix.

Theorem 20 (Classification of unital *-homomorphisms). Let \(A\) and \(B\) be unital finite-dimensional complex C*-algebras with fixed block presentations as above. Unital *-homomorphisms \(\varphi \colon A \to B\), up to unitary conjugacy in \(B\), correspond bijectively to multiplicity matrices \(R\) from \(A\) to \(B\). The correspondence sends \(\varphi\) to the rank matrix \(k^{(i)}_j = \operatorname{rank}(\varphi(p_i) q_j)\), where \(p_i\) is the \(i\)-th block projection of \(A\) and \(q_j\) the \(j\)-th block projection of \(B\). Two unital *-homomorphisms are unitarily conjugate in \(B\) if and only if their multiplicity matrices agree.

Proof. Let \(\varphi \colon A \to B\) be a unital *-homomorphism. Set \(e_i = \varphi(p_i)\); these are orthogonal projections of \(B\) with \(\sum_i e_i = 1\), and each \(e_i\) commutes with \(Z(B)\), so \(e_i = \bigoplus_j e_i^{(j)}\) with \(e_i^{(j)} \in M_{m_j}(\mathbb{C})\) a projection. Put \(k^{(i)}_j = \operatorname{rank}(e_i^{(j)})\); then \(\sum_i k^{(i)}_j = m_j\).

The restriction \(\varphi_i \colon p_i A p_i \to e_i B e_i\) is a unital *-homomorphism from \(M_{n_i}(\mathbb{C})\) onto a subalgebra of \(\bigoplus_j M_{k^{(i)}_j}(\mathbb{C})\), hence it is a tuple of *-homomorphisms \(\psi^{(i)}_j \colon M_{n_i}(\mathbb{C}) \to M_{m_j}(\mathbb{C})\) with \(\psi^{(i)}_j(1) = e_i^{(j)}\). A nonzero *-homomorphism out of a simple algebra is injective, and an injective *-homomorphism \(M_{n}(\mathbb{C}) \to M_{m}(\mathbb{C})\) with \(\psi(1)\) of rank \(k\) exists only when \(k = n r\) for some \(r \geq 0\), in which case \(\psi(M_n)\) is unitarily conjugate inside \(M_m\) to \(M_n \otimes 1_r\) acting on \(\mathbb{C}^n \otimes \mathbb{C}^r\). Thus \(k^{(i)}_j = n_i r^{(i)}_j\) for a unique \(r^{(i)}_j \in \mathbb{N}\).

Conversely, given a multiplicity matrix \(R\), choose for each \(j\) a decomposition \(\mathbb{C}^{m_j} \cong \bigoplus_i (\mathbb{C}^{n_i} \otimes \mathbb{C}^{r^{(i)}_j})\); the summands have total dimension \(\sum_i n_i r^{(i)}_j = m_j\), so the decomposition exists. Define \(\varphi\) blockwise by letting \(p_i A p_i\) act on the \(i\)-th summand and as zero elsewhere. This is a unital *-homomorphism with rank matrix \(k(R)\).

Unitary conjugacy preserves ranks of projections, so distinct multiplicity matrices give non-conjugate homomorphisms. Two homomorphisms with the same multiplicity matrix are conjugate: for each \(j\) choose a unitary of \(M_{m_j}\) carrying the chosen decomposition of \(\mathbb{C}^{m_j}\) of the first homomorphism to that of the second; the direct sum of these unitaries conjugates the homomorphisms in \(B\). ◻

Corollary 21 (Outer automorphisms). Let \(A = \bigoplus_{i=1}^{k} M_{n_i}(\mathbb{C})\). Automorphisms of \(A\), modulo inner automorphisms, are classified by permutations of the blocks with equal dimension vector entries. Concretely, \(\mathrm{Aut}(A)/\mathrm{Inn}(A)\) is isomorphic to the group of permutations \(\sigma\) of \(\{1, \dots, k\}\) with \(n_{\sigma(i)} = n_i\) for all \(i\).

Proof. By 20 a unital *-endomorphism of \(A\) is a multiplicity matrix \(R\) with \(\sum_i n_i r^{(i)}_j = n_j\) for all \(j\). An automorphism is invertible, so the induced map on \(K_0\), which sends the \(i\)-th basis element to \(\sum_j r^{(i)}_j\) times the \(j\)-th, is a permutation matrix; hence \(R\) is a permutation matrix \(\delta_{j, \sigma(i)}\) with \(n_i = n_{\sigma(i)}\). Every such \(R\) is realized by the homomorphism permuting equal blocks, and such homomorphisms are outer unless \(\sigma\) is the identity and the homomorphism is inner. ◻

Example 22 (Two homomorphisms into \(M_3\)). Take \(A = \mathbb{C} \oplus M_2(\mathbb{C})\) and \(B = M_3(\mathbb{C})\). A multiplicity matrix is a pair \((r^{(1)}, r^{(2)}) \in \mathbb{N}^2\) with \(1 \cdot r^{(1)} + 2 \cdot r^{(2)} = 3\). The solutions are \((3, 0)\) and \((1, 1)\). There are exactly two conjugacy classes of unital *-homomorphisms \(A \to M_3(\mathbb{C})\): one in which the \(M_2\) summand acts as zero, and one in which the scalar summand acts on a one-dimensional summand and the \(M_2\) block acts on the remaining \(\mathbb{C}^2\). The classification is exact, small, and verifiable by hand.

The theorem and its corollary give a restricted existence-and-uniqueness package for morphisms. The restriction is essential: the classification is of *-homomorphisms of observable algebras up to unitary conjugacy in the codomain. Nothing is claimed about morphisms of states, channels, or dynamics, where the later sections show that the analogous uniqueness statements fail.

Modules and factorization

The factorization construction of the admissible list has a complete finite-dimensional form: modules over a decomposed algebra decompose into blocks.

Proposition 23 (Module factorization). Let \(A = \bigoplus_{i=1}^{k} M_{n_i}(\mathbb{C})\) and let \(M\) be a finite-dimensional left \(A\)-module. Then \(M = \bigoplus_{i=1}^{k} p_i M\) uniquely as \(A\)-modules, each summand \(p_i M\) is a module over the simple block \(M_{n_i}(\mathbb{C})\), and \(p_i M \cong (\mathbb{C}^{n_i})^{\oplus r_i}\) for a unique multiplicity \(r_i \geq 0\). Two modules are isomorphic if and only if their multiplicity vectors agree.

Proof. The projections \(p_i\) are central and orthogonal with \(\sum_i p_i = 1\), so \(M = \bigoplus_i p_i M\) as vector spaces, and each summand is stable under \(A\) because \(p_i\) is central. A module over \(M_{n_i}(\mathbb{C})\) is semisimple with unique simple module \(\mathbb{C}^{n_i}\), so \(p_i M \cong (\mathbb{C}^{n_i})^{\oplus r_i}\) with \(r_i = \dim p_i M / n_i\) determined by the module. Isomorphism of modules preserves the central idempotent decomposition and the multiplicities, and conversely equal multiplicity vectors give isomorphic modules. ◻

The proposition is the representation-theoretic face of 20: the multiplicity vector of a module is the column of a multiplicity matrix in the endomorphism case. It also states the uniqueness of factorization in the module category, and the uniqueness is total in this regime: unlike the multipartite state decompositions of the next section, the module decomposition of a semisimple algebra has no nonunique cases.

Remark 24 (\(K\)-theoretic bookkeeping). The multiplicity vector of a module, the dimension vector of an algebra, and the rank matrix of a homomorphism are three views of the same combinatorics. Formally, the Grothendieck group \(K_0(A)\) of a unital finite-dimensional complex C*-algebra is the free abelian group on the \(k\) simple classes, \(K_0(A) \cong \mathbb{Z}^k\), the tensor product induces the pairwise product of basis indices via \(M_{n_i} \otimes M_{m_j} \cong M_{n_i m_j}\), and a unital *-homomorphism acts on \(K_0\) by the transpose of its multiplicity matrix: the image of a minimal projection of block \(i\) has rank \(r^{(i)}_j\) in block \(j\). The classification of 20 is therefore a statement about morphisms of free abelian groups with nonnegativity constraints, which is one reason the result is computable.

Uniqueness of decomposition

This section collects the positive uniqueness statements available in the finite-dimensional setting and states, at the level the evidence supports, where uniqueness stops.

Where uniqueness holds

Proposition 25 (Uniqueness in the restricted classes). Uniqueness of decomposition up to permutation of isomorphic factors holds in each of the following restricted classes.

  1. Unital finite-dimensional complex C*-algebras under direct sum: 11.

  2. Unital *-homomorphisms of such algebras up to unitary conjugacy: 20.

  3. Tensor factorizations into prime-dimensional factors: if a finite-dimensional Hilbert space is written as a tensor product of factors of prime dimension, the multiset of factor dimensions is unique up to permutation, by unique prime factorization of the total dimension.

  4. Bipartite pure states of a finite-dimensional bipartite system, up to the Schmidt decomposition: the Schmidt coefficients are unique, and the left and right Schmidt subspaces are unique as subspaces.

The third and fourth items are the tame corners of quantum theory: a fixed tensor structure with finitely many factors. Factorizations into composite dimensions are not unique, as \(\mathbb{C}^2 \otimes \mathbb{C}^6 \cong \mathbb{C}^3 \otimes \mathbb{C}^4\) shows, so the third item restricts to prime dimensions on purpose. The fourth is the strongest state-level uniqueness statement available without further structure, and it fails as soon as the state is multipartite with more than two factors or mixed, as the next subsection records.

Where uniqueness fails

Remark 26 (Multipartite and mixed states). Tensor decomposition of a multipartite pure state is not unique in general: distinct sets of factor vectors can realize the same state, and the minimal number of product terms is not a complete invariant. For mixed states the situation is worse, as the following proposition records. Any decomposition claim about states must therefore name its class, and the restricted classes of 25 are the ones the evidence supports.

Proposition 27 (Ensemble nonuniqueness). Every mixed density operator \(\rho\), that is, every density operator of rank at least two, admits infinitely many distinct decompositions into pure states. Pure states are the only exceptions: a rank-one projector is an extreme point of the state space and admits no nontrivial decomposition.

Proof. Let \(\rho = \sum_{k=1}^{r} \lambda_k |v_k\rangle\langle v_k|\) be the spectral decomposition with \(r \geq 2\) and all \(\lambda_k > 0\). Every choice of an isometry \(U \colon \mathbb{C}^{r} \to \mathbb{C}^{m}\) with \(m \geq r\) produces a pure-state ensemble of \(\rho\) through the unitary-mixing construction: the unnormalized vectors \(|\phi_j\rangle = \sum_{k=1}^{r} U_{jk} \sqrt{\lambda_k} |v_k\rangle\) for \(j = 1, \dots, m\) satisfy \(\sum_j |\phi_j\rangle\langle\phi_j| = \rho\), because \(U^* U = I_r\) rearranges the spectral sum. The corresponding ensemble consists of the normalized pure states \(|\phi_j\rangle/\|\phi_j\|\) with probabilities \(p_j = \|\phi_j\|^2\). Varying \(U\) over the unitary group gives continuously many distinct ensembles, and two ensembles related by no unitary mixing differ as decompositions. In the qubit case \(\rho = I/2\) the construction reads \(I/2 = \frac{1}{2}(|0\rangle\langle 0| + |1\rangle\langle 1|) = \frac{1}{2}(|+\rangle\langle +| + |-\rangle\langle -|)\), and every orthonormal basis of \(\mathbb{C}^2\) gives a new decomposition. ◻

The proposition is the state-level counterpart of the normal-form warning: every mixed state has a canonical spectral decomposition, and no decomposition into pure states is unique. Uniqueness returns only in the restricted bipartite case of 25.

Remark 28 (Interacting systems). Interacting physical systems need not admit unique decompositions. Entanglement, emergent phenomena, gauge redundancy, and nontrivial interactions each break naive decomposability: a gauge-invariant state does not split along the subsystem decomposition of the underlying Hilbert space; a coupled system’s observable algebra need not be the tensor product of the factor algebras; and emergent degrees of freedom need not appear as factors of any microscopic decomposition. Existence-and-uniqueness theorems are realistic only in carefully restricted subclasses: finite-dimensional quantum systems with fixed tensor structure, finite groupoid models, and selected classes of topological field theories. This paper proves uniqueness in the first of those regimes at the level of observable algebras and morphisms and claims nothing for the others.

Remark 29 (Gauge redundancy). Under a gauge quotient, the decomposition of a system depends on the choice of subalgebra retained after reduction. Two systems with isomorphic reduced observable algebras can carry non-isomorphic unreduced data, and the reduction map does not preserve tensor decompositions in general. Decomposition after quotient is therefore a different problem from decomposition before quotient, and the admissible construction list marks quotient as a construction whose contribution to \(\mathcal{C}\) must be stated case by case.

Normal forms and channels

Normal form theorems describe every object of a class in a canonical way without classifying the objects up to isomorphism. The distinction is sharp in the theory of completely positive maps, and this section states the results with the distinction in force.

Stinespring dilation

Theorem 30 (Stinespring). Let \(A\) be a unital C*-algebra, \(H\) a Hilbert space, and \(\Phi \colon A \to B(H)\) completely positive. Then there exist a Hilbert space \(K\), a *-representation \(\pi \colon A \to B(K)\), and a bounded operator \(V \colon H \to K\) with \(\Phi(a) = V^* \pi(a) V\) for all \(a \in A\). Among such dilations the minimal one, in which \(K = \overline{\pi(A) V H}\), is unique up to a unitary intertwiner.

The construction [16] is the GNS construction adapted to a completely positive map. On the algebraic tensor product \(A \otimes H\) define the semidefinite form \[\langle a \otimes \xi, a' \otimes \xi' \rangle_\Phi = \langle \xi, \Phi(a^* a') \xi' \rangle,\] quotient by the null space, and complete to obtain \(K_0\) with \(\pi(a)(b \otimes \xi) = ab \otimes \xi\) and \(V \xi = 1 \otimes \xi\). Complete positivity is exactly the condition that makes the form positive semidefinite and \(\pi\) multiplicative; for general positive maps the construction breaks at this step. Minimality replaces \(K_0\) by the closed span \(\overline{\pi(A) V H}\), and any two minimal dilations are intertwined by a unitary, because both realize the same GNS pair up to the universal property of the construction. The theorem is a normal form for completely positive maps: every completely positive map has a canonical representation-theoretic envelope, and the minimal envelope is unique.

Kraus form and its status

In finite dimension the dilation theorem has a matrix form.

Theorem 31 (Kraus normal form). Every completely positive map \(\Phi \colon M_n(\mathbb{C}) \to M_m(\mathbb{C})\) can be written \(\Phi(X) = \sum_{s=1}^{r} K_s X K_s^*\) with Kraus operators \(K_s \colon \mathbb{C}^n \to \mathbb{C}^m\). The minimal number \(r\) of Kraus operators equals the rank of the Choi matrix \(J(\Phi) = \sum_{i,j} E_{ij} \otimes \Phi(E_{ij})\). Two Kraus families \((K_s)_{s=1}^{r}\) and \((L_t)_{t=1}^{r}\) of minimal size represent the same map if and only if \(L_t = \sum_s u_{ts} K_s\) for a unitary \(u \in U(r)\).

Proof. The Choi matrix [3], [10] of a completely positive map is positive semidefinite, and a spectral decomposition \(J(\Phi) = \sum_{s=1}^{r} \lambda_s v_s v_s^*\) with \(\lambda_s > 0\) yields Kraus operators \(K_s = \sqrt{\lambda_s} \, \mathrm{mat}(v_s)\) of minimal count \(r = \operatorname{rank}(J(\Phi))\), where vec stacks the columns of a matrix and mat is its inverse; conversely any Kraus family of size \(r\) produces \(J(\Phi) = \sum_s \mathrm{vec}(K_s) \mathrm{vec}(K_s)^*\) of rank at most \(r\). Equality of two maps is equality of their Choi matrices, and two positive semidefinite factorizations \(J = \sum_s v_s v_s^* = \sum_t w_t w_t^*\) of the same rank are related by a unitary change of frame \(w_t = \sum_s u_{ts} v_s\). Unpacking \(\mathrm{vec}\) gives the statement on Kraus operators. ◻

Minimal Kraus rank does not separate isomorphism classes of channels. Every unitary channel has minimal Kraus rank one, and distinct unitaries give non-isomorphic channels with fixed-point algebras of different dimensions (32). Non-minimal dilations inflate the presented family size without changing the map. Kraus rank therefore classifies minimal dilations of individual maps and nothing more.

Example 32 (Equal count, different channels). Both \(\Phi(X) = X\) and \(\Psi(X) = Z X Z\) on \(M_2(\mathbb{C})\), with \(Z\) the Pauli \(Z\) operator, have minimal Kraus count one. They are not unitarily equivalent channels: the fixed-point algebra of \(\Phi\) is all of \(M_2(\mathbb{C})\), of complex dimension four, while the fixed-point algebra of \(\Psi\) is the diagonal algebra \(\mathbb{C} \oplus \mathbb{C}\), of dimension two, and unitary conjugation preserves the dimension of the fixed-point algebra. The invariant \(r = 1\) does not separate the two channels.

Channels as morphisms

Channels are the morphisms of the quantum setting: completely positive trace-preserving maps composed in sequence and combined in parallel. The normal form composes poorly. The minimal Kraus count of a composition satisfies \(\operatorname{rank}(J(\Psi \circ \Phi)) \leq \operatorname{rank}(J(\Phi)) \cdot \operatorname{rank}(J(\Psi))\) by the factorization of the Choi matrix through the composition, and the inequality is strict in general, so the normal-form invariant does not even add along composition. Parallel composition multiplies counts on the tensor product. No classification of channels up to isomorphism follows from the normal form, and no such classification appears in the literature on open quantum systems. The normal form delivers a canonical minimal realization of each individual map.

Remark 33 (Inflation under dilation). A single channel arises from dilations of every environment dimension above the minimal one: adjoining an ancilla of dimension \(a\) to a minimal realization of rank \(r\) produces a family of Kraus operators of size \(r + a\) describing the same map. The ambient count is therefore not an invariant of the channel at all; only the minimal count is, and the minimal count is an invariant of the normal form.

Reconstruction from constituents

Classification asks which invariants separate objects. Reconstruction asks which probes determine an object. The two patterns are distinct in status and in content, and reconstruction is the better developed of the two.

Commutative C*-algebras

Theorem 34 (Gelfand-Naimark). Every C*-algebra admits a faithful isometric *-representation on a Hilbert space [7]. In the commutative case, every unital commutative C*-algebra is isometrically *-isomorphic to \(C(X)\) for a compact Hausdorff space \(X\) given by its character space, and \(X\) is recovered from \(A\) as the set of multiplicative linear functionals with the weak-* topology.

The theorem is a representation and reconstruction result. In the commutative case it is also a classification: commutative unital C*-algebras are classified by their spectra. In the noncommutative case it is not a classification, and the block decomposition of 11 is what classifies the finite-dimensional noncommutative objects. The contrast is the structural lesson: reconstruction of an object from its probes is a different achievement from a list of isomorphism classes, and the first pattern is the one that generalizes.

Symmetry data from superselection categories

The second reconstruction template recovers symmetry data from categorical data.

Theorem 35 (Doplicher-Roberts reconstruction). Let \(\mathcal{C}\) be a symmetric tensor *-category with conjugates, direct sums and subobjects, with \(\mathrm{End}(\mathbf{1}) = \mathbb{C}\), satisfying the selection criteria of the DHR analysis of a local net of observable algebras [4]. Then \(\mathcal{C}\) is equivalent to the category of finite-dimensional unitary representations of a compact supergroup \(G\), and \(G\) is unique up to isomorphism [5], [6].

The hypotheses travel with the theorem and are part of its statement: symmetric structure, conjugates, direct sums, subobjects, \(\mathrm{End}(\mathbf{1}) = \mathbb{C}\), and the DHR selection criteria, which in particular involve Haag duality and exclude long-range forces in their standard form. The reconstruction is a decomposition-and-recovery template: the superselection sectors compose into a tensor category, and the symmetry group is read back from the category.

The uniqueness clause is where hypotheses bite. When the setting is relaxed from the symmetric DHR situation to a braided Galois one, the representation category of the fixed-point theory is not determined by the original category alone, and additional data, expressible as soliton representations, are required [12]. The uniqueness in 35 therefore does not survive hypothesis change, and any use of the theorem outside its stated hypotheses is conditional.

Remark 36 (Probes and classification). The reconstruction pattern is an instance of the general principle that objects can be probed by the morphisms into or out of them. A standing qualification governs any argument of this form: Yoneda does not, by itself, establish physical equivalence, empirical adequacy, or a classification theorem. Faithfulness of a probe functor is a mathematical statement about a category. Whether the probes carry physical content depends on data outside the functor.

Admissible constructions and closure

The construction list of the framework section becomes computable in the finite-dimensional setting: the effect of each construction on dimension vectors is arithmetic.

Proposition 37 (Construction arithmetic). Let \(A\) and \(B\) be unital finite-dimensional complex C*-algebras with dimension vectors \(\mathbf{n}(A)\) and \(\mathbf{n}(B)\). Then:

  1. \(\mathbf{n}(A \oplus B) = \mathbf{n}(A) \uplus \mathbf{n}(B)\), the multiset union.

  2. \(\mathbf{n}(A \otimes B) = \{ n m : n \in \mathbf{n}(A),\, m \in \mathbf{n}(B) \}\), the pairwise product multiset.

  3. \(A \oplus B\) is irreducible under direct sum if and only if one summand is zero and the other is simple.

Proof. Direct sum concatenates block lists and matrix algebras satisfy \(M_n(\mathbb{C}) \otimes M_m(\mathbb{C}) \cong M_{nm}(\mathbb{C})\), which gives the two computations. The third clause restates simplicity of the blocks: a direct sum is reducible exactly when both summands are nonzero, and the irreducible objects are the simple algebras \(M_n(\mathbb{C})\). ◻

Corollary 38 (Closure of a finite set of blocks). Let \(S = \{M_{n_1}(\mathbb{C}), \dots, M_{n_k}(\mathbb{C})\}\) and let \(\mathcal{C} = \{\oplus, \otimes\}\). The closure \(\langle S \rangle_{\mathcal{C}}\) is the class of unital finite-dimensional complex C*-algebras whose dimension vector entries lie in the multiplicative semigroup generated by \(n_1, \dots, n_k\) in \(\mathbb{N}_{\geq 1}\).

Proof. By 37 every element of the closure has dimension vector entries built from the \(n_i\) by multiplication, and every finite multiset of such products is realized by taking direct sums of tensor products of generators. ◻

Example 39 (Qubit closure). For \(S = \{ \mathbb{C}, M_2(\mathbb{C}) \}\) under direct sum and tensor product, the multiplicative semigroup generated by \(1\) and \(2\) is \(\{1, 2, 4, 8, \dots\}\), and the closure is the class of algebras whose dimension vectors have entries that are powers of two. The algebras \(M_3(\mathbb{C})\) and \(M_2(\mathbb{C}) \oplus M_3(\mathbb{C})\) are outside the closure. The enumeration of 13 checks this arithmetic.

The closure of a finite set of blocks under direct sum and tensor product is always infinite as a class, because tensor powers keep producing new sizes, and the closure is always decidable at the level of individual dimension vectors: entry size belongs to a finitely generated multiplicative semigroup of the positive integers, and membership in such a semigroup is decidable. The computability of the closure is a peculiarity of the finite-dimensional regime, where the constructions act on integer vectors; nothing here extends it to closure taken over the full admissible construction list of the framework section.

Worked example: two qubits

A single system runs through every construction of this volume and shows what each layer of the machinery returns.

Observable-algebra decompositions do not extend to the underlying state space of a two-qubit system. Let \(P\) have observable algebra \(\mathcal{O} = M_4(\mathbb{C})\) and state space the density operators of \(\mathbb{C}^4\), with both tensor product and direct sum named as composition operations. Under direct sum, \(\mathcal{O}\) is simple with dimension vector \(\{4\}\), so the algebraic system is irreducible; the endomorphism multiplicity data of 21 give a trivial outer automorphism group. Under tensor product, \(\mathcal{O} \cong M_2(\mathbb{C}) \otimes M_2(\mathbb{C})\) factors into two qubit blocks of dimension vector \(\{2\}\). Restricting to \(M_2(\mathbb{C}) \oplus M_2(\mathbb{C})\) inside a superselection model produces two sectors whose state space is the join of two Bloch balls by 17. At the state level, bipartite pure states of two qubits have unique Schmidt decompositions, while mixed states have no unique decomposition into pure states: the maximally mixed state \(I/4\) admits continuously many pure-state ensembles by 27. The algebraic layer supports a complete existence-and-uniqueness theory for observable algebras and morphisms; the state layer in the mixed regime admits no unique representation into extremal components.

Remark 40 (The generation identity). The structural generation statement of the program, that every admissible system is of the form \(\mathcal{F}(I_1, \dots, I_n)\) with \(I_i\) irreducible, is proved in the restricted regime of this section: every unital finite-dimensional complex C*-algebra is a direct sum of irreducible blocks, the construction \(\mathcal{F}\) is the direct sum, and the irreducibles are the \(M_n(\mathbb{C})\). The identity \(\mathrm{Phys}_{\mathrm{adm}} \simeq \langle \mathfrak{F} \rangle_{\mathcal{C}}\) for general admissible systems remains conjectural, and nothing in this volume upgrades its status.

Decomposition and morphisms

The morphism layer of the schema distinguishes coarse-grainings, embeddings, dualities, equivalence morphisms, and limiting procedures. Decomposition interacts with each of them differently, and two of the interactions are exact.

Proposition 41 (Embeddings and multiplicity matrices). In the setting of 20, a unital *-homomorphism \(\varphi \colon A \to B\) is injective if and only if its multiplicity matrix has no zero row. Injective unital *-homomorphisms are the embeddings of the finite-dimensional C*-category.

Proof. The restriction of \(\varphi\) to the \(i\)-th block is a *-homomorphism out of the simple algebra \(M_{n_i}(\mathbb{C})\) with unit \(e_i = \varphi(p_i)\). If the \(i\)-th row of the multiplicity matrix is zero then \(e_i = 0\) and \(\varphi\) kills the \(i\)-th block. If some entry \(r^{(i)}_j\) is positive then \(e_i \neq 0\) and the homomorphism is injective on the simple block, because the kernel of a *-homomorphism out of a simple algebra is zero or everything. ◻

Proposition 42 (Spectra of direct sums). Let \(A\) and \(B\) be unital commutative C*-algebras with spectra \(X\) and \(Y\). Then \(A \oplus B \cong C(X \sqcup Y)\) and the spectrum of a direct sum is the disjoint union of the spectra.

Proof. Characters of \(A \oplus B\) are unital *-homomorphisms to \(\mathbb{C}\). The identity splits as \(1 = 1_A + 1_B\) with \(1_A 1_B = 0\), so \(\chi(1_A) = \chi(1_A)^2\) in \(\mathbb{C}\) and \(\chi(1_A) \in \{0, 1\}\): every character restricts to a character of exactly one summand and vanishes on the other. The character space is therefore the disjoint union of the character spaces of the summands, and the Gelfand isomorphism \(A \oplus B \cong C(X \sqcup Y)\) follows. ◻

Coarse-grainings behave in the opposite way. A coarse-graining can merge blocks and forget the decomposition: the map \(M_2(\mathbb{C}) \oplus M_2(\mathbb{C}) \to M_2(\mathbb{C})\) sending \((x,y)\) to \((x+y)/2\) is positive and unital, and it destroys the superselection decomposition of its domain. Irreducibility is not preserved by coarse-graining, and a decomposition statement about coarse-grained systems must be justified for the target system, not inherited from the source.

Limiting procedures are the least controlled interaction. Direct sums of finite-dimensional blocks pass to direct integrals of factors in infinite-volume or continuum limits, and the block structure of the approximating algebras need not survive the limit. The decomposition theorems of this volume are finite-dimensional statements; their behavior under the limiting procedures named in the construction list is a separate problem, and nothing here claims continuity of decomposition under limit.

Dualities commute with decomposition in the commutative case by 42, and Doplicher-Roberts reconstruction (35) recovers symmetry data from decomposed sector categories. Equivalence morphisms preserve decomposition when they are *-isomorphisms of observable algebras, and only for those is the dimension vector invariant.

Wildness and the parameterization boundary

The finite-dimensional decomposition theorem classifies its objects by a finite invariant. The expectation that similar parameterizations exist beyond tame regimes is false, and the falsity is a theorem.

Theorem 43 (Tame-wild dichotomy). Every finite-dimensional algebra over an algebraically closed field is either derived tame or derived wild [1]. Derived wildness is preserved under degeneration, and derived tameness is preserved under deformation.

Theorem 44 (Wildness of the generic determinant). The maximal Cohen-Macaulay module theory of the generic determinant is wild [2]: already in rank two, the isomorphism classes of modules cannot be parametrized by a finite-dimensional variety over the coefficients.

The phrase used for this phenomenon in the literature is that the representation theory is quite wild: the wildness is not merely an absence of known invariants, it is the proven absence of finite-dimensional parameterization. The technique behind the dichotomy is the theory of matrix problems, in which representations are reduced by an algorithm to normal forms for boxes and arrows; derived tameness means the reduction terminates with a one-parameter-family description of indecomposables, and derived wildness means the reduction embeds an arbitrary matrix problem, after which no finite-dimensional parameterization can exist. A parallel obstruction in equivariant stable homotopy [8] shows that no meaningful classification of compact \(C_p\)-equivariant \(H\underline{\mathbb{F}}_p\)-modules at odd primes is to be expected, so the boundary is not confined to representation theory of algebras.

Corollary 45 (Boundary on decomposition programs). Any program that decomposes systems into irreducibles and then parametrizes the irreducibles by finite-dimensional data is confined to tame regimes. In wild regimes the decomposition step may still exist while the parameterization step is provably unavailable.

The corollary is the volume’s impossibility content. It does not contradict 11, which lives in the tamest possible regime: finite-dimensional algebras over \(\mathbb{C}\) with semisimple radical-free structure. It marks the edge of the pattern. The tame-wild distinction also appears algorithmically: group isomorphism for extensions with tame quotient admits polynomial-time algorithms whose bounds fail in the wild case [9], so the boundary is visible to computation as well as to parameterization.

For the structural generation program the consequence is precise. A generation theorem of the form \(P \simeq \mathcal{F}(I_1, \dots, I_n)\) is achievable where the irreducibles \(I_i\) come from a tame class with finite or parametric invariants. Where the irreducibles are wild, the generation step may still succeed while the classification of the generators fails, and the long-term target \(\mathrm{Phys}_{\mathrm{adm}} \simeq \langle \mathfrak{F} \rangle_{\mathcal{C}}\) inherits the restriction. The candidate subclasses for existence-and-uniqueness theorems, finite-dimensional quantum systems, finite groupoid models, and selected classes of topological field theories, are all tame in this sense, and the tameness of each candidate must be made explicit wherever the candidate is used.

Counterexamples and boundary cases

Three natural strengthenings of the results above fail, and the failures locate their hypotheses.

Uniqueness without parameterization

A tempting inference runs: decomposition is unique, therefore the irreducibles can be parametrized. The inference is false in general. Finite-dimensional algebras over an algebraically closed field satisfy the Krull-Schmidt theorem, so every such algebra decomposes uniquely into indecomposable summands up to permutation, and 44 shows that in wild regimes the indecomposables still admit no finite-dimensional parameterization. Unique decomposition and parameterization of the pieces are different achievements; the finite-dimensional C*-case of 11 has both only because its indecomposables are the matrix algebras \(M_n(\mathbb{C})\), indexed by a single integer.

The C*-hypothesis

Drop the C*-structure and 11 fails at the first step. The dual numbers \(\mathbb{C}[x]/(x^2)\) form a two-dimensional algebra with Jacobson radical the span of \(x\), which is nonzero; the algebra is indecomposable and not simple. 9 used positivity through the spectral theorem, and without positivity nothing forces the radical to vanish. Boundary case: the class of unital finite-dimensional complex C*-algebras is exactly the class where the block decomposition with matrix blocks holds; widening to finite-dimensional algebras requires the semisimplicity hypothesis, and widening to infinite-dimensional C*-algebras replaces direct sums by the direct-integral machinery of the contrast theorem.

Normal-form invariants

A second tempting inference runs: every map has a canonical normal form with an invariant, therefore the maps are classified. 32 refutes it: two channels with minimal Kraus count one differ on a single matrix, and the invariant cannot see the difference. The general lesson is that an invariant of a normal form is not an isomorphism invariant of the classified objects. The decomposition theorems of this volume avoid the trap because their invariants are complete: the dimension vector decides isomorphism of algebras (12), and the multiplicity matrix decides conjugacy of homomorphisms (20). Completeness is the property the normal forms lack.

Interacting systems

The counterexample search found no interacting system outside the restricted subclasses with a uniqueness theorem, and none is claimed here. The failure modes named earlier, entanglement, gauge redundancy, emergence, and coupling, are open problems rather than refuted conjectures: no theorem states that interacting systems admit unique decompositions, and existence-and-uniqueness theorems are realistic only in restricted subclasses. Any analysis that needs uniqueness for an interacting system must restrict the class and prove the restriction suffices.

Computational evidence

Three computational artifacts accompany this paper. They are evidence about the combinatorics of the theorems above; no statement in this volume is established by them.

The first artifact is an executable taxonomy layer in Haskell. Its module declares instances of the shared family, construction, and decomposition classes for the systems treated here: matrix blocks and their direct sums, tensor products of blocks, and Kraus normal form data. Multiplicity matrices appear as data types mirroring 19. The names mirror the definitions of this paper and the mechanization targets listed with this paper. The Haskell layer is computational evidence, validated against kernel-checked specifications through cross-audit, and it is not verified by any proof assistant.

The second artifact is a set of enumeration experiments in Python. One experiment enumerates dimension vectors of the closure of \(\{\mathbb{C}, M_2(\mathbb{C})\}\) under direct sum and tensor product up to total dimension \(64\) and checks the arithmetic of 37. It returns \(1085\) dimension vectors, all of whose entries lie in the multiplicative semigroup \(\{1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 32, 64\}\) generated by \(1\) and \(2\), and it confirms that \(M_3(\mathbb{C})\) lies outside the closure. A second experiment enumerates multiplicity matrices for the three block pairs of 1 and returns exactly \(2\), \(1\), and \(6\) solutions, matching the conjugacy-class counts of the table entry by entry. A third computes Choi ranks of completely positive maps: for a random map of minimal rank \(1\), presented Kraus families of sizes \(1\), \(2\), \(3\), \(5\), and \(8\) all return Choi rank \(1\) and reproduce the same map, while the identity channel and conjugation by the Pauli \(Z\) operator both have rank \(1\) and differ on the Pauli \(X\) operator. The experiments run in seconds on ordinary hardware and record their seeds in the source.

The third artifact is a code evidence table, given in the appendix, that maps each computational claim to the file and function that exhibits it.

Limitations

The classification package of this volume has four sharp limits.

Uniqueness is a restricted phenomenon. The existence-and-uniqueness statements hold for unital finite-dimensional complex C*-algebras and their *-homomorphisms, and for the tame corners of tensor decomposition named in 25. For interacting systems only restricted-subclass realism is available: unique decomposition is not assumed anywhere in this paper.

Normal forms are not classifications. The Stinespring and Kraus results describe maps canonically; they do not classify channels up to isomorphism, and no such classification is on record.

The wildness results of 44 confine finite-dimensional parameterization to tame regimes, and the tame-wild boundary for the representation categories of physical interest, as opposed to generic algebras, is unstudied.

The structural generation identity is conjectural, the admissibility conditions that would define \(\mathrm{Phys}_{\mathrm{adm}}\) are unfixed, and no result here depends on either. Doplicher-Roberts reconstruction is available only under hypotheses that include symmetric structure and the DHR selection criteria; relaxing them destroys the uniqueness clause.

No part of the mathematical content of this volume is machine-checked.

Two premises are unfixed, and no theorem above depends on them. The admissibility conditions defining \(\mathrm{Phys}_{\mathrm{adm}}\) do not exist yet, so every statement quantified over admissible systems is conditional. The equivalence \(\simeq\) for physical systems is open as well; this volume uses *-isomorphism of observable algebras and claims nothing about coarser identifications. Probe-based arguments carry a standing qualification, that Yoneda does not, by itself, establish physical equivalence, empirical adequacy, or a classification theorem; no reconstruction result here is read as a classification result.

Conclusion

Irreducibility is relative to the composition data of a system, and the relativity is visible in concrete examples: one algebra is irreducible for the direct sum and reducible for the tensor product. Within the tamest regime, unital finite-dimensional complex C*-algebras, the decomposition theory is complete: blocks exist, the dimension vector classifies, and unital *-homomorphisms are classified by multiplicity matrices with explicit arithmetic constraints. That package is exactly as large as advertised: it classifies observable algebras and their morphisms, not states, dynamics, or composition.

Beyond the tame regime the pattern breaks in three directions, each with a theorem behind it. Normal forms do not classify: Kraus rank is an invariant of the normal form and not an isomorphism invariant of channels. Interacting systems admit no unique decomposition outside restricted subclasses. Wild representation categories forbid finite-dimensional parameterization of their indecomposables. A program that assembles systems from fundamental families by admissible constructions must therefore state its regime, and the regime that admits existence-and-uniqueness theorems is restricted, tame, and finite.

Reconstruction from probes generalizes better than classification by invariants: commutative C*-algebras are recovered from their spectra, and symmetry data are recovered from superselection categories under hypotheses that cannot be dropped. Whether the probe pattern can be unified with the reconstruction theorems into a single principle is open. What is established now is a division of labor: classification where the invariant is finite or parametric, reconstruction where probes suffice, and impossibility theorems where neither holds.
